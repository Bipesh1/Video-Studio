Ideas that passed the topic finder (`prompts/fine-print-topics.md`, v5.0) and are not made yet, best first. Every fact was checked against the sources given; "sure" means a published paper or official estimate says it, "fairly sure" means good sources agree but the number is an estimate or debated. When a video is made from one, move it to the finder's list of earlier videos and delete it here.

## 1. Is the you of ten years ago still you?

- **Kind:** about you
- **Thumbnail:** the mascot beside a ghostly copy of his younger self, bits of him flaking off and being replaced. Words: SOME OF YOU NEVER CHANGES
- **The promise:** you will know which parts of you are brand new, and which are as old as you are.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot looks at a childhood photo; tiny pieces of him float off and new ones float in. "Your body is replacing itself right now, but some of you has never been replaced."
- **The catch:** your body replaces about 330 billion cells every day (about one in a hundred), mostly blood and gut, but most of the neurons of your cortex and the core of your eye lenses are as old as you are.
- **The journey:** from the photo into the body: the blood (cells streaming in and out), the gut lining (peeling and renewing), then the brain (still) and into the eye, to the lens's core, made around birth.
- **Facts:**
  - About 330 billion cells turn over a day, nearly 90% of them blood cells; about 80 grams of cells a day. Sender and Milo, Nature Medicine, 2021 ([Weizmann](https://wis-wander.weizmann.ac.il/node/11205), [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/our-bodies-replace-billions-of-cells-every-day/)). Sure.
  - No noticeable new neurons in the adult cortex, dated by the carbon-14 of nuclear tests. Spalding, Frisén and others, Cell, 2005 ([Alzforum](https://alzforum.org/node/37786)); new neurons do form in one small part of the hippocampus (Cell, 2013, [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/nuclear-bomb-tests-reveal-formation-of-new-brain-cells-15014)). Sure.
  - The proteins in the core of the eye lens form around birth, with only a very small renewal after. Lynnerup and others, PLoS ONE, 2008 ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/3f589a192f9d4e3b8cab7419a441232e)). Sure.
- **Now you'll notice:** looking someone in the eye, you are looking at a part of them as old as they are.
- **Jokes:** "your gut lining has been through more renovations than your kitchen"; the mascot trying to return his old cells.
- **Comments:** "so I'm not legally the same person who owes that money"; "the eye part gave me chills"; "what about teeth?"
- **Scores:** 4, 5, 5, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5

## 2. Why can't you remember being a baby?

- **Kind:** about you
- **Thumbnail:** the mascot as a baby inside a locked memory bubble, the grown mascot outside with a key. Words: YOU DID REMEMBER IT
- **The promise:** you will know that your earliest memories were made, and what happened to them.
- **The first ten seconds:** a baby's first birthday, a cake, a party hat; then black. "You were there. So why can't you remember any of it?"
- **The catch:** brain scans show babies of about one already store memories in the hippocampus, much as adults do; what fails later seems to be finding them again, not making them.
- **The journey:** from the birthday party into the baby's head, to the hippocampus lighting up as it sees a face; then years pass and the memory sits there, behind a door the grown-up cannot find.
- **Facts:**
  - 26 babies of 4 months to 2 years in an fMRI scanner; those whose hippocampus was more active on first seeing a picture looked longer at it later, a sign of memory, clearest after about a year. Yates, Turk-Browne and others, Science, March 2025 ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/babies-do-make-memories-so-why-cant-we-remember-them-as-adults), [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=3151986)). Sure.
  - Why they are lost later is not settled; the authors lean to a problem of retrieval, from animal studies. Nobody knows yet.
- **Now you'll notice:** your earliest "memory" may be from a photo you saw later, not the day itself.
- **Jokes:** the baby mascot filing memories in a cabinet the grown mascot has lost the key to.
- **Comments:** "I swear I remember my crib"; "so they're still in there?"; "my first memory is from age 3, normal?"
- **Scores:** 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 4, 5

## 3. What if the Moon disappeared tonight?

- **Kind:** what if
- **Thumbnail:** the night sky with a Moon-shaped hole, the mascot pointing up in shock. Words: THE MOON IS GONE
- **The promise:** you will know what we would lose the next morning, and what we wouldn't.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot looks at the full Moon from his window; it blinks out. "If the Moon vanished tonight, what would you notice first?"
- **The catch:** the tides would shrink to about a third, but the scary part people repeat (the Earth tipping wildly) is not settled: older work said the tilt could swing from 0 to 85 degrees, newer simulations say it would mostly stay within about 10 degrees of now.
- **The journey:** from the window out over the sea at low tide, up to the Earth from space and its tilted spin, then forward through millions of years of seasons.
- **Facts:**
  - The Sun raises tides about 45% as strong as the Moon's, so tides would shrink to roughly a third ([Science ABC](https://www.scienceabc.com/nature/universe/what-would-happen-if-we-had-no-moon), check against a physics source before the script). Fairly sure.
  - Laskar, Joutel and Robutel, Nature, 1993: without the Moon the tilt could be chaotic between about 0 and 85 degrees ([Laskar 1993](https://perso.imcce.fr/jacques-laskar/pdf/Laskar_Joutel_Robutel_1993.pdf)). Sure that they found it.
  - Lissauer, Barnes and Chambers, Icarus, 2011/2012: much smaller swings, typically 20 to 25 degrees over hundreds of millions of years ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/28472-earth-moon-life-oddity.html), [arXiv 1404.7505](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1404.7505)). Sure that they found it; the debate is open.
- **Now you'll notice:** the sea's tide is the Moon pulling on the water you see.
- **Jokes:** the werewolves out of a job; the mascot's moonlit romantic dinner suddenly pitch dark.
- **Comments:** "what about the werewolves"; "I thought we'd all die"; "so the tilt thing was a myth?"
- **Scores:** 5, 4, 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 3

## 4. Why can't you tickle yourself?

- **Kind:** big question
- **Thumbnail:** the mascot tickling his own foot, bored face, while a robot hand tickling him makes him crack up. Words: YOUR BRAIN SAW IT COMING
- **The promise:** you will know the trick your brain plays on every move you make.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot tickles his own ribs: nothing. A feather from someone else: he bursts out laughing. "Same touch. Why does only one of them work?"
- **The catch:** your brain predicts the feeling of every move you make and turns it down; a robot that tickled people's palms the moment they moved felt less tickly than one that waited a fifth of a second.
- **The journey:** into the arm, up the nerve, to the brain, where a "prediction" of the touch races ahead and cancels it; then the delayed robot hand slipping past the prediction.
- **Facts:**
  - Self-made touch rated less tickly than the same touch from a robot; adding a delay of up to 200 ms made it more tickly. Blakemore, Wolpert and Frith, Nature Neuroscience, 1998 and 1999 ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-cant-a-person-tickle/), [PDF](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/autism/blakemore_frith98_NN.pdf)). Sure.
  - That the cerebellum does the cancelling was challenged by a later study (Shergill and others, 2013) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6975290)). Say "scientists still argue where".
- **Now you'll notice:** you can't surprise yourself; the same trick is why your own voice sounds quieter to you.
- **Jokes:** the mascot hiring a robot to tickle him; trying to sneak up on his own hand.
- **Comments:** "I can tickle myself though?"; "tagging my brother who's immune"; "the delay thing is wild".
- **Scores:** 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 4, 4

## 5. How long would it take to fall through the Earth?

- **Kind:** scale / what if
- **Thumbnail:** the mascot jumping into a hole in the ground, the Earth cut open with a glowing core and a timer. Words: 38 MINUTES
- **The promise:** you will know how long, and the strange thing that happens in the middle.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot at the edge of a hole in his garden that goes straight through the planet; he jumps. "How long until you come out the other side?"
- **The catch:** about 38 minutes, not the 42 in old textbooks, because the Earth's heavy core pulls you faster than a plain ball of rock would; you'd fly fastest at the centre, where you'd weigh nothing, and stop just at the far side's surface.
- **The journey:** down through crust, mantle, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core, a speedometer climbing, weightless at the centre, slowing up the far side to peek out of a hole on the other side of the world.
- **Facts:**
  - 38 minutes 11 seconds with a realistic Earth, against 42 minutes 12 seconds for an even one. Klotz, American Journal of Physics, 2015 ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/50312-how-long-to-fall-through-earth.html), [arXiv 1308.1342](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.1342)). Sure.
  - The deepest hole ever dug is about 12 km (the Kola Superdeep Borehole) ([Labroots](https://labroots.com/trending/chemistry-and-physics/671/how-long-to-fall-through-earth-new-answer-to-old-problem/amp)). Sure.
  - Not counting air, heat or the Earth's spin: say so.
- **Now you'll notice:** the ground under you is 6,000 km of rock down to a core as hot as the Sun's surface.
- **Jokes:** the mascot packing snacks for the trip; arriving upside down in someone's garden.
- **Comments:** "what about the lava?"; "I'd come back out the other side and fall back forever"; "Kola hole mention!"
- **Scores:** 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 3

## 6. Are you more bacteria than human?

- **Kind:** big question / everyday catch
- **Thumbnail:** the mascot split down the middle, one half human cells, one half bacteria, a scale between them. Words: IT'S A TIE
- **The promise:** you will know the real count, and why the famous "ten to one" was wrong.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot looks in the mirror; his reflection is made of wriggling bacteria. "They say you're 90% bacteria. That's not true."
- **The catch:** the old "ten bacteria for every human cell" came from a rough guess in 1972; a careful count in 2016 found about 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells: roughly a tie.
- **The journey:** into the gut, where nearly all the bacteria live, counting them; then through the blood, where most of your own cells are red blood cells; the two piles on a balance.
- **Facts:**
  - 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells in a 70 kg reference man, the bacteria uncertain by about half; red blood cells are most of the human cells. Sender, Fuchs and Milo, PLOS Biology, 2016 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4991899), [Eppendorf](https://www.eppendorf.com/gb-en/beyond-science/health-medicine/bacteria-versus-body-cells-a-11-tie/)). Sure.
  - The 10 to 1 came from Luckey, 1972, extrapolated from the gut. Sure.
- **Now you'll notice:** every trip to the toilet changes the score.
- **Jokes:** a toilet flush tipping the balance toward "human".
- **Comments:** "so I'm half me"; "I knew that 90% thing was fake"; "who's in charge then?"
- **Scores:** 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 4

## 7. Could the whole human race fit in a sugar cube?

- **Kind:** scale
- **Thumbnail:** a sugar cube on a spoon with tiny people inside, the mascot staring at it. Words: ALL 8 BILLION OF US
- **The promise:** you will see how empty you really are.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot drops a sugar cube into his tea. "Everyone alive could fit in this."
- **The catch:** an atom is almost all empty space; squeeze it out of every person alive and the 500 million tonnes of humanity would fill about two sugar cubes.
- **The journey:** from the tea into the sugar, down into one atom of a person, its tiny nucleus with the cloud of electrons around it, then the empty space squeezed out until everyone fits on the spoon.
- **Facts:**
  - About 8.3 billion people at about 62 kg each: about 5 × 10^11 kg ([Science ABC](https://www.scienceabc.com/pure-sciences/can-the-entire-human-race-fit-inside-a-sugar-cube)). Fairly sure.
  - At the density of an atom's nucleus (about 2.3 × 10^17 kg per cubic metre) that is about 2 cubic centimetres; a sugar cube is 3 to 5 grams, about 2 to 5 cubic centimetres. Claude's own arithmetic, matching the sources. Fairly sure.
  - The "squeezing" is a picture of the numbers, not something physics allows: say so ([Bad Astronomy](https://www.syfy.com/index%2ephp/syfy-wire/follow-up-the-volume-of-humanity-if-all-the-space-in-our-atoms-is-removed)).
- **Now you'll notice:** when you touch something, your atoms never really touch it.
- **Jokes:** the cube weighs as much as all the world's cruise ships; the mascot deciding not to sugar his tea after all.
- **Comments:** "don't eat that"; "so I'm mostly nothing"; "how heavy would it be?"
- **Scores:** 5, 5, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 4

## 8. How many people have ever lived?

- **Kind:** deep time
- **Thumbnail:** an endless crowd fading into the distance, the mascot small in front, circled. Words: YOU ARE ONE IN 117 BILLION
- **The promise:** you will know how many came before you, and how rare it is to be alive right now.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot alone on an empty plain; behind him, row upon row of people appear, back to the horizon. "Everyone who has ever been born, standing behind you."
- **The catch:** about 117 billion people have been born; about 7 of every 100 are alive today.
- **The journey:** back through time along the crowd: today's billions, cities, the first farms, the first humans 200,000 years ago, a few thousand; then forward to the mascot in front.
- **Facts:**
  - About 117 billion people born by 2022, about 6.8% alive in 2022; an estimate, "part science and part art", from 190,000 BCE. Population Reference Bureau, 2022 ([PRB](https://www.prb.org/articles/how-many-people)). Sure that it is their estimate; the number is rough.
  - About 4 billion more births expected by 2050. PRB. Fairly sure.
- **Now you'll notice:** for most of history, very few people got to live as long as you will.
- **Jokes:** the queue for the bathroom; the mascot waving to his great-great-great... grandparents.
- **Comments:** "I thought there were more alive than dead"; "7% is crazy"; "so many forgotten names".
- **Scores:** 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 4, 5

## 9. Somewhere in the universe, is there a copy of you?

- **Kind:** about you / scale
- **Thumbnail:** two identical mascots waving at each other across a vast starfield. Words: HE'S REALLY OUT THERE?
- **The promise:** you will know why some physicists think there is, and how far away.
- **The first ten seconds:** the mascot waves at the camera; far away across space, a second identical mascot waves back. "If space goes on forever, there may be another you, doing exactly this."
- **The catch:** if space is infinite and much the same everywhere, there are only so many ways atoms can be arranged in a region, so they must repeat; Max Tegmark worked out that the nearest copy of you would be about 10 to the 10 to the 28 metres away, a number with more zeros than there are atoms in the universe.
- **The journey:** from the mascot out past the Earth, the galaxy, the galaxies, the edge of what we can see, then on and on until the same Earth, the same room, the same mascot appear.
- **Facts:**
  - "About 10^10^28 metres from here" (Scientific American, May 2003), 10^10^29 in the paper. Tegmark, "Parallel Universes" ([arXiv astro-ph/0302131](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0302131), [the 2003 article](https://stanford.edu/~oas/SI/QM/papers/SAMultiverse603.pdf)). Sure that he wrote it.
  - It rests on space being infinite and fairly uniform, which nobody knows: say so.
- **Now you'll notice:** the universe may be big enough to repeat itself.
- **Jokes:** the copy is about to make the same bad decision; "is he better at his job than me?"
- **Comments:** "hope mine has a better life"; "infinity is scary"; "does he also skip leg day?"
- **Scores:** 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 5, 4, 5

## 10. Why does time feel faster as you get older?

- **Kind:** about you
- **Thumbnail:** a clock whose hands blur faster as a child mascot grows into an old one. Words: THE YEARS SHRINK
- **The promise:** you will know the three best explanations, and how to slow your own years down.
- **The first ten seconds:** a child's summer holiday stretching on forever; then an adult's year flashing past in a blink. "Why did a year feel endless at seven, and so short now?"
- **The catch:** nobody knows for sure: one year is a smaller share of a longer life; fewer new things means fewer memories to measure by; and one engineer argues the ageing brain takes in fewer "frames" a second.
- **The journey:** a timeline of the mascot's life, each year a slice of the whole shrinking; into memory, sparse with routine days, dense with new ones; into the eye, its quick movements gathering pictures.
- **Facts:**
  - Well documented, no agreed cause ([Harvard SITN](https://sites.harvard.edu/sitn/?p=15855)). Sure.
  - The share-of-life idea goes back to Paul Janet, 1877 (via William James). Sure.
  - Bejan, European Review, 2019: slower processing of images with age ([Big Think](https://bigthink.com/mind-brain/why-time-speeds-up), [The New Daily](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/life/2019/03/24/why-time-flies-when-we-age)). Sure that he argued it; it is one view.
  - Novelty and memory ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-speed-of-life/202307/new-views-of-why-age-accelerates-time)). Fairly sure.
- **Now you'll notice:** a week of new places feels longer looking back than a month of routine. Not advice, just how memory works.
- **Jokes:** the mascot's birthdays arriving faster and faster like a stack of cakes.
- **Comments:** "this year went by in a week"; "so travel slows time"; "that's why summer felt like forever".
- **Scores:** 5, 3, 5, 5, 5, 5, 4, 5
