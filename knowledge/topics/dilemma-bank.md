## 1. Monkeys Refuse Unfair Pay

- **Kind:** the big questions of human nature (why unfairness makes us furious)
- **The game:** fairness: the same instinct as the ultimatum game.
- **Opening (0 to 3 s):** a monkey hands a pebble to a scientist, gets a slice of cucumber, and throws it back at her. "This monkey just went on strike."
- **Second line:** "The cucumber was fine. What it couldn't stand was what the monkey next to it got."
- **The story:** in 2003 Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal paired capuchin monkeys side by side; each traded a token for food. Happy with cucumber, until the neighbour got a grape for the same token. Then they refused more and more trades, and when the neighbour got grapes for doing nothing at all, they refused most of all.
- **The cast plays it:** the Saint and the Cheat in two cages; the Saint's cucumber, the Cheat's grape; the Saint's face; then a crowd of people in the arena turning down unfair shares (the ultimatum game).
- **The mirror:** the colleague with the same job and a bigger pay; the sibling who got more. "The fury isn't greed. It's older than us."
- **Hopeful ending:** that fury is what keeps groups fair.
- **Sources:** Brosnan and de Waal, "Monkeys reject unequal pay", _Nature_ 425, 2003 (trade completion fell from about 95% to about 50%, and to about 20% when the partner got grapes for nothing) ([Nature comment and reply](https://www.nature.com/articles/428140a), [Lawrence Journal-World](https://www2.ljworld.com/news/2003/sep/18/study_monkeys_recognize/)). Sure that they found it; critics argued the monkeys may just want the better food, and the authors' reanalysis answered that ([Brosnan and de Waal 2006](https://www.emory.edu/LIVING_LINKS/publications/articles/Brosnan_deWaal_2006b.pdf)). Say there was a debate.
- **Long-video note:** yes: fairness from monkeys to dogs to children to nations.

## 2. Cancer Is a Cell That Cheats

- **Kind:** the big questions (why does any body hold together?), across scales
- **The game:** the cheater in a public goods game: thirty trillion cells sharing, one that takes without giving.
- **Opening:** a city of glowing cells working in perfect order; one cell starts copying itself and won't stop. "Every cancer starts with one cell that stops playing fair."
- **Second line:** "And your body has been fighting cheaters like it for six hundred million years."
- **The story:** every body is a team of cells that agreed to rules: divide only when told, die when asked, do your job, share the food. Cancer breaks those rules. Researchers found the same cheating in every kind of many-celled life, from fungi to whales, and the same defences.
- **The cast plays it:** the Copycat cells keep the rules; one Cheat cell grabs food and multiplies; the body's police find it.
- **The mirror:** every team, office and country faces the same cheater problem.
- **Hopeful ending:** life has beaten cheaters billions of times; that's why you exist at all.
- **Sources:** Aktipis and others, "Cancer across the tree of life: cooperation and cheating in multicellularity", _Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B_ 370, 2015 ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4581024/), [ASU News](https://news.asu.edu/content/new-study-describes-cancers-cheating-ways)). Sure. Not health advice; no cure claims; handle gently.
- **Long-video note:** yes, very Kurzgesagt: cells, bodies, why big animals don't get more cancer.

## 3. Your Brain Rewards You for Revenge

- **Kind:** the big questions (why does revenge feel good?)
- **The game:** punishing cheaters: groups stay fair only if someone will punish, even at a cost to themselves.
- **Opening:** someone pays real money just to take money away from a stranger who cheated them, and a brain scan lights up. "People pay to punish. And their brains enjoy it."
- **The story:** in 2004 Swiss researchers scanned people who had been cheated in a money game and could punish the cheater. When the punishment really cost the cheater, a reward area of the brain lit up, and the more it lit, the more people were willing to pay to punish.
- **The cast plays it:** the arena with and without punishers: without them, Cheats take over; with a few Saints willing to pay to punish, cooperation holds.
- **The mirror:** the urge to report the queue-jumper, the review left out of spite.
- **Hopeful ending:** the urge for justice is a tool that made large groups of strangers possible; the trick is aiming it well.
- **Sources:** de Quervain and others, "The neural basis of altruistic punishment", _Science_ 305, 2004 ([PDF](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_Revenge.pdf), [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna5820379)). Sure that they found it; a small study, and "the brain enjoys it" is the authors' reading of reward-area activity: say "scientists think".

## 4. Gossip Keeps Groups Honest

- **Kind:** the big questions (why do we gossip?)
- **The game:** reputation: when everyone knows who cheated, cheating stops paying.
- **Opening:** a group of strangers whisper about one man; next round, they all leave him out, and he changes. "Gossip just fixed this group."
- **The story:** in 2014 Stanford researchers had 216 people play a group money game. When players could pass on who had been selfish, and groups could leave people out, the groups cooperated far more, and the people left out came back cooperating as much as anyone.
- **The cast plays it:** the arena, first with no talk (the Cheat wins), then with gossip (the Cheat is found out and learns).
- **The mirror:** online reviews, the group chat, the office.
- **Hopeful ending:** gossip isn't only cruel; it's one of humanity's oldest ways to keep strangers fair (and it can go wrong).
- **Sources:** Feinberg, Willer and Schultz, "Gossip and ostracism promote cooperation in groups", _Psychological Science_ 25(3), 2014 ([Stanford](https://news.stanford.edu/pr/2014/pr-upside-of-gossip-012714.html), [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140127193852.htm)). Sure.

## 5. The Fishermen Who Saved Their Sea

- **Kind:** a true story with a hidden rule
- **The game:** the tragedy of the commons, and how it was beaten.
- **Opening:** fishing boats crowding one spot, nets tangling, a fight on the water. "Every expert said this sea was doomed."
- **The story:** in Alanya, Turkey, too many fishermen fought over the best spots. They drew up a list of the spots and a lottery, and each boat moved to the next spot in turn. The fights stopped and the fishing went on. Elinor Ostrom studied places like it all over the world, forests, fisheries, pastures, and in 2009 became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics.
- **The cast plays it:** the arena as a sea: everyone grabbing (empty sea); then the rotation (full sea).
- **The mirror:** the shared fridge, the office printer, the group project.
- **Hopeful ending:** people are not doomed to wreck what they share; they can make rules together.
- **Sources:** Nobel Prize 2009, Ostrom ([Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/)); the Alanya case from Ostrom, _Governing the Commons_, 1990 ([ASU case study](https://seslibrary.asu.edu/sites/default/files/seslibrary/cases/alanya_coastal_fisheries_iad_final.pdf)). Sure of Ostrom and the rotation; the start year differs between sources (1970s): check in the book.

## 6. You Both Think You Do Most of the Housework

- **Kind:** the experiment that surprised its own scientists
- **The game:** keeping score: each side sees only its own half.
- **Opening:** two notebooks slammed on a table. "Scientists asked 37 couples who does the housework. Their answers added up to more than 100%."
- **Second line:** "And nobody was lying."
- **The story:** in 1979 Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly asked each spouse alone how much of each job they did, from making breakfast to starting arguments. Added together, the two shares came to more than the whole. In early tests, couples compared their answers at home and fought, so the researchers changed the questions.
- **The cast plays it:** the Cheat and the Saint each keeping a notebook of their own half; the viewer sees both fill.
- **The mirror:** work, roommates, group projects: you remember what you did because you were there.
- **Hopeful ending:** "check the other notebook".
- **Sources:** Ross and Sicoly, "Egocentric biases in availability and attribution", _Journal of Personality and Social Psychology_ 37(3), 1979 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.3.322)). Sure. (Remakes the unposted dishes video, true this time.)

## 7. The Day Soldiers Stopped Shooting Each Other

- **The game:** a repeated prisoner's dilemma; tit for tat.
- **Sources:** Axelrod, _The Evolution of Cooperation_, 1984, ch. 4, from Ashworth 1980 ([Wikipedia](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_and_let_live_(World_War_I)>), [Axelrod ch. 4](https://www.gwern.net/docs/economics/1984-axelrod-theevolutionofcooperation-ch4-theliveandletlivesysteminwwi.html)). Sure it happened; the game reading is disputed by some.

## 8. The Traffic Jam That Came From Nowhere

- **The game:** everyone acting for themselves makes a wave no one chose (a coordination failure).
- **Sources:** Sugiyama and others, _New Journal of Physics_ 10, 033001, 2008: 22 cars, a 230 m circle, 30 km/h ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/03/080304075707.htm), [paper](https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/93262/NewJPhys_10_033001.pdf)). Sure.

## 9. They Removed a Highway. Traffic Got Faster.

- **The game:** Braess's paradox: each driver's best choice makes everyone slower.
- **Sources:** Seoul's Cheonggyecheon, 2005 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braess%27s_paradox), [Cornell](https://blogs.cornell.edu/info2040/2016/09/16/seoul-and-braess-paradox)). Fairly sure; find a primary traffic study first.

## 10. Most People Refuse This Free Money

- **The game:** the ultimatum game.
- **Sources:** Güth and others 1982; Henrich and others 2001 across 15 societies ([Wikipedia](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Ultimatum_Game), [notes](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/ultimatum-game-cross-cultural/)). Sure of the pattern; check the figures in the papers.

## 11. How Being Nice Beat Every Cheater

- **The game:** the repeated prisoner's dilemma; Axelrod's 1980 tournaments, tit for tat.
- **Sources:** ([Stanford](https://ee.stanford.edu/~hellman/Breakthrough/book/chapters/axelrod.html)). Sure. Veritasium told it at length; tell it short, played by the cast.

## 12. Why Anger and Guilt Exist

- **The game:** promises (commitment): emotions that make you hard to cheat and easy to trust.
- **Sources:** Robert Frank, _Passions Within Reason_, 1988 ([review](https://www.jasoncollins.blog/posts/franks-passions-within-reason)). A theory, not a measured result: say "one economist argued".
