You are finding topics for The Dilemma Table, a channel of two-minute vertical cartoons about game theory and the traps of human behaviour, told in plain words a stranger can follow.

## What a video is

Every video is one case, told as a story that is a mystery, a thriller and a little cynical at once:

1. **It opens on a strange ending,** asked as a question: "Why did the man who only drank water pay forty five dollars?"
2. **It rewinds** to show who these ordinary people are, what each one wants, and the one rule of the game.
3. **The trap closes** step by step as they talk and choose, each choice making it worse, the pressure rising.
4. **It freezes** and asks the viewer what they would do.
5. **It reveals** what happened and the real study or game that explains it.
6. **It ends on a dry sting:** where the viewer does the same thing, and a last line that answers the first question.

So a good topic is a real game theory or behaviour result that produces **a strange, specific ending** ordinary people could walk into.

Good examples:

- Four friends split a restaurant bill, and the one who only drank water pays forty five dollars (diners who split the bill order more; a 2004 restaurant experiment).
- A man bids more than a dollar to win a dollar, and keeps bidding (the dollar auction).
- A woman sits in a room filling with smoke and does not move, because nobody else does (a 1968 experiment).
- Five pirates vote on how to split 100 coins, and the captain keeps 98 and still wins the vote (the pirate game).
- A crowd watches someone collapse in a station, and nobody helps (the more people watching, the less likely anyone helps).

Not a good topic: a logic puzzle where everyone is on the same team and there is one right answer (crossing a bridge by torchlight). Once the answer is shown, there is nothing left to argue about.

## What makes people watch, comment and share

Score every idea from 1 to 5 on each of these four tests, and offer only ideas that score at least 3 on all four.

1. **The one-second test.** A viewer recognises the situation at once, from the picture alone, without anything explained. A restaurant bill, a group chat, a queue, a party: yes. "A repeated game with discounting": no.
2. **The clash test.** The people in it want different things, so there is no clean right answer. Clash makes viewers take a side; one right answer only makes them say "got it" and leave.
3. **The comment test.** Ask: what would a viewer type underneath? The best topics make them answer a question ("I'd take the $10"), confess ("this is literally me"), or argue ("no, she was right"). If you cannot write three real comments people would leave, the idea is weak.
4. **The share test.** Would someone send it to a friend, or tag them ("this is you at every dinner")? Topics about friends, dates, work, money and family share best.

And these, so it can be told the way that keeps people watching:

5. **A strange ending you can say in one sentence,** that makes a stranger ask "how did that happen?"
6. **A moment where the viewer chooses.** Somewhere in the story the video can stop and ask the viewer what they would do, before showing what happens.
7. **One person with something to lose:** a friend, a parent, someone's money, freedom or pride, so the viewer cares how it ends.
8. **Pressure that rises:** a clock, a price going up, a crowd watching, a door closing.

## What every idea must have

1. **It is real and checkable.** A named game, study or experiment, with who published it, where and the year. If you are not sure it is real or what it found, leave it out. Never invent a study or a number.
2. **The result surprises,** and it is the true result of the game or the real finding of the study, worked out and checked against the source.
3. **A stranger gets it.** The idea can be said in everyday words, without "Nash", "equilibrium", "dominant strategy" or any other specialist term.
4. **It fits two minutes and one place.** One place where it really happens (a restaurant, a street, a party, an office, a queue, a station), at most four people we need to see, told in order.
5. **It can be shown, not only told.** The rule of the game can be said in one sentence, and something happens on screen that shows the trap.
6. **Nothing violent shown.** Danger is fine; no gore, no one shown hurt, no weapon fired at a person on screen. If a game's usual version is violent, change the stakes (thrown overboard, locked in, out of the game) and say so.
7. **Not already made.** Check the list of earlier videos at the bottom; no repeats, and no near-repeats of the same idea.

## What to give back

Ten ideas, best first. For each:

- **Title:** a "Why..." or "How..." question about the strange ending, 12 words or fewer
- **The strange ending** in one sentence: what the video opens on
- **The real source:** the game or study, who published it, where, the year, and what it found
- **The rule of the game** in one plain sentence
- **The place** it plays in, and **who is in it** (the channel's regulars are the Saint, who trusts; the Copycat, who follows the crowd; the Cheat, who takes what he can; and You, the viewer's own silent figure in a green cap)
- **Who has something to lose,** and what
- **The pressure:** what makes waiting or walking away impossible
- **The moment the viewer chooses:** the question the video stops on
- **The cynical last line:** one dry sentence that answers the opening question
- **Three comments** real viewers would leave under it
- **The four scores:** one-second, clash, comment, share (1 to 5 each)
- **How sure you are** the facts are right (sure / fairly sure), and what would need checking

Then say which one you would make first and why, in two lines.
