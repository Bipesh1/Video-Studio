You are finding topics for What's the Catch?, a channel of two-minute vertical animated explainers about the surprise hiding inside something everyone uses, sees or believes every day: why cinema popcorn costs nine dollars, why your phone's map only works because time runs faster in space, why the Amazon is not really the Earth's lungs. Each video is told by one narrator in plain words a stranger can follow, while the camera travels through one drawn world: from the thing in your hand, out or in to where the catch is, opening things up on the way.

## What a good topic is

**Something the viewer touched, saw or believed today, with a surprise inside it that flips what they were sure of.** The catch can be said in one sentence that makes a stranger say "wait, what?" and want to tell someone.

It can be about money, science, space, the body, food, history or the home. Good examples:

- Your phone's map only works because clocks run faster in space; without a correction for it, your blue dot would be kilometres off within a day (money: none; science: Einstein, on everyone's phone).
- The Amazon makes a lot of oxygen but uses almost all of it up itself; tiny plants in the sea make about half the oxygen we breathe.
- Much of the gold in your ring was made when two dead stars smashed into each other, long before the Earth existed.
- Cinema popcorn costs nine dollars because the ticket money mostly goes to the film studio, so the snacks pay for the cinema.
- When dinosaurs were alive, a day was shorter, because the Moon has been slowly braking the Earth.

Not a good topic: something many people learned at school (why the Moon doesn't fall, why the sky is blue), something nobody's day touches (a far-off galaxy, a rare animal), a scandal about one company, an investing tip, anything that changes by the week, or anything you cannot back with a real, published source.

## What makes people watch, comment and share

Score every idea from 1 to 5 on each of these five tests, and offer only ideas that score at least 3 on all five.

1. **The one-second test.** A viewer recognises the thing at once from a picture alone: a phone map, a gold ring, a popcorn bucket, a sunset, a glass of water.
2. **The "wait, what?" test.** The catch flips something most people believe, and they want to check it or tell someone. If most viewers already know it, it is weak.
3. **The comment test.** What would a viewer type underneath? The best topics make them argue ("that can't be right"), add something they know, or tag someone. If you cannot write three real comments people would leave, the idea is weak.
4. **The share test.** Would someone send it to a friend ("did you know your phone does this?")? Things in everyone's hand, food, space and the body share best.
5. **The journey test.** Can the camera travel to the catch in one continuous move, and open something up on the way? From your phone up to the satellites and into one of them; from your ring back in time to two stars colliding; from a forest down into a single leaf. A topic that can only be told with charts is weaker.

## What every idea must have

1. **Real, checkable facts.** Each from a named source: the scientists or agency who measured it (NASA, a university, a published paper), a regulator, or a company's own report. Say who published it and the year. If a famous number is disputed, say so. Never invent one.
2. **Plain words.** The catch can be said without specialist words unless the same sentence says what they mean.
3. **Fair to everyone.** Explain how it works; do not accuse a named company of anything a source does not say.
4. **One world to draw.** The video travels through one drawn world (the Earth and its satellites, a cinema, the inside of a planet, a body), with no more than four or five things to draw that the studio does not have yet.
5. **It holds for two minutes.** There is enough to explain in six short steps: the question, the moment, follow the clue, the catch, the proof, now you'll notice.
6. **Not already made.** Check the list of earlier videos at the bottom; no repeats and no near-repeats.

## What to give back

Ten ideas, best first, at least three of them not about money. For each:

- **Title:** a "Why..." or "How..." question about the everyday thing, 12 words or fewer
- **The catch in one sentence**
- **The journey:** where the camera starts (in the viewer's hand), where it travels, and what it opens up
- **The clue path:** three to five steps that explain how it really works
- **The facts and numbers** you would use, each with its source (who, what, year) and how sure you are (sure / fairly sure)
- **Now you'll notice:** what the viewer will see differently afterwards (or, for money, the one thing they can do)
- **Three comments** real viewers would leave under it
- **The five scores:** one-second, "wait, what?", comment, share, journey (1 to 5 each)

Then say which one you would make first and why, in two lines.
