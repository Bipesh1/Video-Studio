## 0. Before you write: the package

The strongest creators decide the idea, the title and the thumbnail before they make anything, and drop an idea they cannot package. Write these first, and only write the script if a stranger would click:

```
TITLE: (plain words, 70 characters at most; what the video answers or reveals)
THE QUESTION IN A STRANGER'S HEAD: (when they read the title)
THE PROMISE: (what they will know, or see differently, by the end)
THE ANSWER IN ONE SENTENCE: (the payoff the last line gives)
THUMBNAIL: one big subject (close, bright, what the eye lands on first) + up to six big words that add to the title and never repeat it
THE FIRST TEN SECONDS: (what we see and hear: the title's promise proved at once)
WHY A STRANGER CLICKS: (one sentence; "it is interesting" is not a reason)
KIND: everyday / what-if / big-question
```

Read the title and the thumbnail's words on their own, with no picture: if they do not say what the video is about, rewrite them. Curiosity is good; a promise the video does not keep is not.

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who knows nothing about the subject, must understand every sentence the first time they hear it, and see it explained on screen at the same moment.**

If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly. No specialist words ("margin", "relativity", "orbit decay", "photosynthesis") unless the same sentence says what they mean in everyday words.

## 2. What the video is

An animated explainer of an interesting topic, with good drawings and animation. **Mostly a vertical Short of about 1 minute (a little longer is fine, up to about 1 minute 20), about 145 to 170 spoken words (our narrator is calm, about 2.4 words a second); the surprise by the middle, one twist kept for the end.** A long YouTube video (4 to 6 minutes, 600 to 900 words) only when Biku asks for one. Three kinds of topic:

- **Money:** why cinema popcorn costs nine dollars, why airlines sell more seats than the plane has.
- **Science and space:** why your phone's map only works because time runs faster in space, why the Amazon is not really the Earth's lungs, why the gold in your ring came from two dead stars colliding.
- **Anything else** that passes the same test: the body, food, history, the home.

The test for every topic: **the viewer has touched it today, and the catch flips something they were sure about.** "Why doesn't the Moon fall down?" fails: many learned the answer at school, and nobody's day depends on it. "Your phone's map needs Einstein" passes: everyone used a map today and almost nobody knows it.

**The third kind: the big question everyone has.** What happens when you fall into a black hole? What is in your blood right now? Why do we dream? The biggest explainer videos are questions like these (space, the body, death, how the world really works). Its test: **almost everyone has wondered it, and the answer holds a surprise even for people who think they know.**

**The second kind: the big "what if".** A wild question about you, your body or the human race, answered with real science: could humans evolve to never sleep, if you never cut your hair how long would it grow, could a person live to two hundred. Its test: **it starts from something every viewer does or has every day, and the answer is never a plain yes or no.** The catch is the twist in the answer: the hidden price ("yes, but..."), the built-in limit ("no, and the reason is stranger than you think"), or the place in nature where it already happens (dolphins rest one half of the brain at a time). Not a what-if: superpowers or science fiction with no research behind it, scare stories, anything that reads as health advice.

- **One narrator** tells it, like a friend letting you in on something: curious, a little amazed, never smug, in the same tone every video. Nobody else speaks.
- **Humour that grows out of the topic.** A small joke every 20 to 30 seconds: a silly but true comparison, an absurd consequence, the character's reaction. Jokes about the subject help people remember; jokes dropped in from nowhere do not. Never at the cost of a fact.
- **The camera travels.** The best videos of this channel are one journey through one drawn world: from the blue dot on your phone, up through the sky, out to the satellites, into one of them. Things open up to show their inside. The viewer should feel they went somewhere, not that they watched slides.
- **Every line has one picture**, which builds up piece by piece while the line is spoken. The channel's own character (being designed with Biku; until then, simple faceless figures) reacts, tries things and gets into trouble for the viewer: write what he does in round brackets.
- **One explanation at a time, in order.** The viewer never sees a number before it is said, or a thing before it is named.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Never a fragment such as "Popcorn. Ninety percent profit."
2. **One step to a line.** Each line explains one thing, and that one thing is what its picture shows. If a line needs two pictures, it is two lines.
3. **Name the first piece in the first few words**, so the picture never waits empty while the line gets going.
4. **Name every piece in the words.** If the picture shows a satellite, the line says "satellite". If a number is "38", the line says "thirty eight". This is how each piece knows when to appear.
5. **Every number says what it is a number of, and how big that is in everyday terms.** "Thirty eight millionths of a second a day", then what that means: "light travels more than eleven kilometres in that time".
6. **Say numbers the way people say them**, and write them that way in the spoken line: "nine dollars", "about thirty", "more than half". The picture shows them as figures.
7. **One idea to a sentence,** up to about 18 words. A line takes about 2 to 6 seconds to say.
8. **Every "it", "they" and "this" has an owner the viewer has just heard.**
9. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each step is caused by the one before.
10. No em dashes. No emoji. Mark one important word a line with asterisks for the captions: `Up there, time runs *faster*.`

## 4. The shape: chosen for the topic

Topics differ, so the shape does too (Biku, 2026-10-21: "don't always follow a rigid structure, be flexible according to the topic"). Only three things are fixed:

1. **The hook comes first.** Within the first three seconds, the first line states the question or the wild fact in plain words over the thing itself, so the viewer knows what the video is about. No greeting, no logo, no "in this video".
2. **The last line answers the first.** Then end one of two ways, whichever suits the topic: lead back into the first line so the Short plays round again, or widen out to everyone and leave wonder ("billions of people go through this, and we are only beginning to understand it").
3. **Every step is shown as it is said**, in plain words, with its facts true and sourced.

Between them, pick the shape that explains this topic best. Some that work:

| Shape                   | Good for                                             | How it goes                                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Journey                 | Where something comes from, what is inside something | The camera travels from the everyday thing in, out or back in time, opening things on the way (the water older than the Sun) |
| Loops                   | A big question with several parts                    | Three small questions, each answered at once, building to the big answer                                                     |
| Myth, then truth        | Something everyone believes                          | What everyone thinks, why it seems right, the clue that breaks it, what is really going on                                   |
| Follow the money        | Why something costs what it costs                    | Where each part of the price goes, then the trick                                                                            |
| Step by step            | How something works                                  | The process in order, each step a picture, the surprising step saved for last                                                |
| What if, then the price | A wild "what if" about you                           | The wish, what it would take, the hidden cost or the place it already happens                                                |
| Countdown or comparison | Sizes, records, extremes                             | From small to unbelievable, or two things side by side until the difference lands                                            |
| Before and after        | A change in history or the body                      | How it was, the moment it changed, how it is now                                                                             |

**What every 1-minute Short does** (from two Kurzgesagt Shorts, 2026-10-22):

- **One thing, followed in steps the viewer can count.** Say the steps so they can be shown as "1/3, 2/3, 3/3", with a clear "done" near the middle; then turn to what it means for you.
- **Signpost every turn out loud,** in plain words a stranger follows: "Here's the bad news.", "So what's the good news?", "But there's a catch." Talk to the viewer now and then: "right?"
- **One gauge carries the story:** a number counting down, a bar filling or draining, or the steps. Name it in the layout (section 9) and say its changes in the words, so it moves on them.
- **Things get faces and the character gets one gag** (he argues, scribbles, fails, tries again), written in round brackets where it happens.
- **Science first, then you,** then the twist or the dark side, then hope or wonder.

Mix them when the topic asks (a journey with a myth at its start). Keep a small laugh about every half minute, and keep the picture moving. The studio's beats (the question, the moment, follow the clue, the catch, the proof, your move) are a menu for the middle, in any order; only the hook and the payoff hold their places.

**A long YouTube video (only when Biku asks): 4 to 6 minutes, 600 to 900 words.** The same six steps, larger: a hook of 10 to 20 seconds (within three seconds the viewer knows what it is about, by ten the title's promise is proved), a moment of 20 to 30 seconds with one open loop, three or four loops over 2 to 4 minutes (each opens a smaller question, builds it, answers it, and ends a little before the viewer expects), the catch, the proof (what nobody knows yet, said plainly), the payoff. Mark the 30 to 50 seconds that work alone as a Short with `[SHORT FROM HERE]` and `[SHORT TO HERE]`.

Keep the beat names in the delivered script as THE HOOK, THE MOMENT, THE LOOPS, THE CATCH, THE PROOF, THE PAYOFF.

The first line must be a real question or a plain wild fact that the last line answers. It must not start with "In this video", "Did you know", "Have you ever wondered" or "Imagine".

**In a what-if,** the hook is the what-if itself, about "you" ("Could your body ever learn to live without sleep?"); the loops travel into what the thing really does (into the sleeping brain); the catch is the twist (the price, the limit, or where it already happens); the payoff answers plainly ("So could you evolve to never sleep? Not with a brain like yours.").

## 5. The pictures: one after each line

After each spoken line, on its own line, write the picture in square brackets. Every piece marked `(on: word)` appears when that word is spoken; choose a word that is in the line. Pieces without `(on: ...)` appear one after another.

### The journey pictures (use these for most lines)

They keep the camera moving through one drawn world. The studio draws the world for each video, so describe it in plain words and list it under NEEDS DRAWING.

- `[FLY: up from the phone, through the clouds, to the whole Earth | show: satellites (on: satellites)]`
  The camera travels, without a cut, from wherever the last picture was to the new place: closer in, further out, round, along, down. Say where it ends and what arrives on the way.
- `[OPEN: the satellite | inside: the clock (on: clock)]`
  A thing opens up to show its inside, as if cut away or lifted off: a planet peeled back to its layers, a satellite's shell lifting off its clock, a phone's back sliding off to its chip.
- `[CALLOUT: 38 | caption: MILLIONTHS OF A SECOND / A DAY | at: the satellite clock | on: thirty]`
  A number or two or three words appear inside the picture, pointing at a thing in it, without leaving the world. Up to 10 characters for the number and 28 letters for the caption.

### The explainer cards (for the money in a money video, or one big comparison)

- `[SCENE: cinema counter | sign: POPCORN $9 | show: machine (on: cinema), bucket (on: popcorn), hand with $10 (on: nine)]`
  An illustrated place. Drawn so far: the cinema counter and its auditorium, the coffee counter with its tip screen. Any other place goes under NEEDS DRAWING.
- `[NUMBER: $9 | caption: FOR ONE BUCKET | picture: bucket | count up | on: paid]`
- `[RECEIPT: title: WHAT GOES IN | CORN 35¢ (on: corn) | BUCKET 45¢ (on: bucket) | TOTAL $1.35]`
- `[SPLIT: title: WHERE YOUR / $9 GOES | THE CINEMA $7.65 85% (on: cinema) | BUTTER 55¢ 6% | CORN 35¢ 4%]`
- `[FLOW: title: FOLLOW THE / MONEY | YOU (on: your) -> THE CINEMA (on: goes) -> THE FILM STUDIO (on: studio)]`
- `[BARS: title: WHAT THE CINEMA / KEEPS | ONE BUCKET 7.65 "$7.65" (on: bucket) | ONE TICKET 6 "$6" | red: ONE BUCKET]`
- `[TAG: $4.50 | note: EACH, SHARED | was $9 (on: costs) | on: share]`
- `[QUEUE: 4 people | sign: $9 | on: every]`

Every picture may have a title: one or two short lines at the top (a "/" splits them), up to 24 letters each.

**Make it immersive:**

- Prefer the journey pictures. A science video should be almost all FLY, OPEN and CALLOUT: one world, one camera, from the first line to the last.
- The camera goes from the everyday to the hidden: start on the thing in the viewer's hand, then go up, in, down or through it to where the catch is, then come back to the hand at the end.
- Open things up at least once: the moment the inside is shown is the moment viewers remember.
- **Never stuck on one picture.** Every new fact brings its own movement: the camera flies somewhere, something opens, or a thing arrives and moves. A picture that only waits while the narrator talks for more than about three seconds is a problem (Biku: "it should not be stuck in one photo for long time. each new info should bring a great animation").
- Show scale by moving through it (from your hand to the whole Earth in one move), not by telling it.
- When a card is needed (a money split, a comparison), keep it to one or two in a video, and say `(from: thing)` on it when it grows out of a thing already on screen.

Do not write: characters, faces or dialogue; more than one picture for one line; real logos or brand names in the pictures; a real company accused of anything a source does not say.

## 6. The facts

- Build the video on real, checkable facts and numbers. Every number is the source's own. If it is rounded, say "about", "nearly" or "more than". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- Science: use what the scientists who measured it say, not what websites repeat. When a famous number is disputed, say what is certain instead. A simplification is fine; a wrong picture is not.
- A number that changes by country or year says which ("in the US in 2025").
- Do not claim a named company does something unless a source says so.
- End with SOURCES: who published it, the title and the year, and one line on what it says. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.
- In a money video, the tip in step 6 is legal, safe and something a person can really do.
- In a what-if, the question can be wild but every answer is real. Say what is known as known, what scientists think as "scientists think", and what nobody knows as "nobody knows yet". A guess about the future is never told as a fact, and a number about the far future is given only with whose estimate it is.
- A what-if never gives health advice: never tell the viewer to sleep less, eat less, hold their breath or try anything on themselves.

## 7. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

1. Do I know what or whom every line is about?
2. Is it a whole sentence?
3. Is there any specialist word I would have to already know?
4. Is every piece of the picture named in its line's words?
5. After step 3, could I tell a friend how it works?
6. Would I want to tell a friend the catch?
7. Does the last line answer the first?
8. Would the first line and picture alone stop me swiping?
9. Is there a small laugh at least every half minute, and does each one come from the topic?
10. In a what-if: is every answer real, and is what nobody knows said plainly?

If any answer is no, rewrite that line more plainly.

## 8. A finished example (the opening)

```
1. THE HOOK
NARRATOR: How does the blue dot on your map know exactly where you are?
[FLY: close on a phone in a hand, its map with a blue dot | title: HOW DOES THE / BLUE DOT KNOW?]
NARRATOR: The answer is twenty thousand kilometres above your head, and it bends *time*.
[FLY: up from the phone, through the sky, to the whole Earth | show: satellites (on: twenty)]

2. THE MOMENT
NARRATOR: Most people think a satellite is watching their phone.
[FLY: closer to the Earth, a satellite turning its eye toward the city]
```
