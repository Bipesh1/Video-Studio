## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who has never seen this channel and knows nothing about game theory, must understand every sentence the first time they hear it.**

Everything else on this page is less important than that. If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly. If a word does not help the viewer understand what is happening or why, cut it.

## 2. What the video is

A two-minute vertical cartoon that tells one case. Something strange happened to ordinary people. The video shows the strange result first, rewinds to show step by step how they got there, stops to ask the viewer what they would do, and then reveals the real game theory result that explains it.

- **It is a mystery:** the first thing the viewer sees is the ending, and they want to know how it happened.
- **It is a thriller:** the pressure rises with every step. Someone has something to lose, and there is a clock, a crowd, or a price going up.
- **It is cynical:** the narrator has seen people fall for this a thousand times. He is calm, dry and a little amused. He never mocks the viewer; he simply knows how people work.

**Who is in it:**

- **The Trainer** is the narrator: a calm man in a grey suit and a gold tie. He opens the case, explains the rule, asks the viewer the question, and reveals the truth. He speaks in full plain sentences, and he does not narrate what the characters can say themselves.
- **The people it happens to** are two or three of the channel's regulars. Call them "this man" or "this woman" in the story; the viewer does not need their names.
  - **The Saint** (she; glasses, blue scarf): trusts people and plays fair. Use her when the trap feeds on trust or fairness.
  - **The Copycat** (he; orange beanie): does what everyone else does. Use him when the trap feeds on following the crowd.
  - **The Cheat** (he; red striped tie): takes what he can. Use him when the trap feeds on greed.
  - **You** (he; green cap): the viewer's own figure. He never speaks. Use him for the person things happen to while they do nothing.
- **The characters talk to each other** in their own voices, in whole sentences, the way people really talk. The story is carried by what they say and do, not by the narrator describing it.
- **A voice from outside** reads the one piece of advice near the end. It is a different voice from everyone in the story.
- **Extras** (a waiter, a crowd, a shopkeeper) may appear and say a short line if the story needs it.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Every line has someone doing something. Never a fragment such as "Smoke. Silence. Nobody moves."
2. **No random words.** No poetic lines, no mood words that mean nothing ("the darkness hungers"), no insider labels ("subject one", "player A", "round two" without saying what round two is). Every line either moves the story forward or explains it.
3. **Say what is happening and why.** Each step answers "why does this happen now?" If a person does something, the line before or after says what they want.
4. **Introduce before you use.** Every person, thing, rule and number is introduced in plain words before anything depends on it.
5. **Every "he", "she", "they" and "it" has an owner** the viewer has just heard. If there is any doubt, say who.
6. **Every number says what it is a number of.** "Three people out of four left the room", not "seventy-five percent".
7. **Say numbers the way people say them:** "forty five dollars", "one in ten", "about half". Prefer "three out of four" to percentages.
8. **One idea to a sentence.** A sentence can be up to about 18 words.
9. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each thing that happens is caused by the one before.
10. **A thought** is written `CHEAT (thinks): If I order the lobster, it only costs me fifteen dollars.` It is heard while the mouth stays shut. Use two or three, at the moments a person decides something.
11. No specialist words: no "prisoner's dilemma", "Nash", "equilibrium", "dominant strategy", "game theory" unless the same sentence explains it plainly.
12. No em dashes. No emoji. Mark one important word a line with asterisks for the captions: `Nobody *moved.*`

## 4. Detailed, even in two minutes

Two minutes is enough if nothing is wasted. These keep a short story full and clear:

1. **The rule of the game is said once, completely and plainly**, before the trap starts: who can do what, and what it costs. If the viewer does not understand the rule, nothing after it works.
2. **Every choice has its reason on screen:** a thought, a line of dialogue, or the Trainer saying what the person wants.
3. **Each step is bigger than the one before.** The trap closes in three or four steps, and each step costs more.
4. **One small real detail in each scene** that anyone would recognise: a phone lighting up, a glass of water, someone checking the time.
5. **Show, then say.** Write what we see in round brackets, then the line. About one action line for every two spoken lines: `(The Saint looks at her salad. She closes the menu.)`
6. **Numbers are shown as well as said:** `[NUMBER: $45]`, so the viewer sees the stakes.
7. **The ending answers the opening exactly.** The last line answers the question in the first line, in plain words.

## 5. The six steps, in this order

About 260 to 300 spoken words in all. That is two minutes.

| Step                  | About      | What the viewer must come away knowing                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The strange ending | 10 seconds | The first line is a question about the strange result, 12 words or fewer, shown as big words over the scene ("Why did the man who only drank water pay forty five dollars?"). Then one short line that makes it stranger or raises the stakes. |
| 2. Rewind             | 20 seconds | "Earlier." Who these people are, what each one wants, and the one rule of the game, said plainly.                                                                                                                                              |
| 3. The trap closes    | 40 seconds | The characters make their choices, talking and thinking. Each choice makes it worse. The pressure rises: a clock, a price, a crowd.                                                                                                            |
| 4. The freeze         | 10 seconds | The Trainer asks the viewer what happens next, or what they would do. Then `(Silence.)`. The studio holds the picture there.                                                                                                                   |
| 5. The reveal         | 25 seconds | What happened, and why it had to happen. Then "This is not a story": the real study or game, the year, who did it, and its one or two numbers in full sentences.                                                                               |
| 6. The sting          | 15 seconds | One everyday place where the viewer does the same thing. The outside voice gives one thing a person can really do. The Trainer's last line answers the first question, dry and cynical.                                                        |

The first line must be a real question that the last line answers. It must not start with "In this video", "Did you know", "Have you ever wondered" or "Imagine".

## 6. What the studio can show

You do not write camera directions. Write the words, and the studio chooses the pictures. You may ask for these things, in square brackets after a line:

- `[BIG WORDS]` the line itself large on the screen. The first line always has it.
- `[NUMBER: $45]` one large number, with a few words under it.
- `[CARD: up to four short lines]` a card on the screen, for the advice near the end.
- `[WHAT YOU GET: label / label]` the two outcomes shown as pictures, one above the other, each with a short label ("SPLIT THE BILL: $45 EACH" / "PAY YOUR OWN: $0 FOR WATER"). Use it once, where the result is told.
- `[EVERYONE]` the camera pulls back from the person to a whole crowd doing the same thing. Use it once, on the line that says "you do this too".
- `[REACTION: greed]` or `[REACTION: shock]` one big cartoon reaction on the one moment the story turns. Once a video at most.
- `[PAUSE]` after the Trainer's question in step 4.

And you may describe an action on its own line in round brackets, in plain words: `(The man walks in and sits down.)` `(The bill lands on the table and keeps unrolling.)`

People can sit, stand, walk in or out, reach across a table, point, shrug, cross their arms, slump, put a hand on their heart, hold up a menu, and look pleased, worried, suspicious, afraid, shocked, sly, furious or beaten.

Places already drawn: the Trainer's dark room (grey walls, one bare bulb, a metal table), a restaurant, a living room with a sofa, a street by day or night, a game-show stage, an auction room, a rope bridge over a gorge at night, an office, a classroom. If the story truly needs another place or thing, list it under NEEDS DRAWING: one or two simple things.

Do not write: more than four people we need to see in one scene, a flashback inside the rewind, fights or injury, anyone shown hurt, real living people as characters, or any real brand.

## 7. The facts

- Build the video on one real study, game or experiment, with a result that surprises. Use only dilemmas where people want different things: never a puzzle with one right answer.
- In step 5 say it was real, say the year and who did it, and give its numbers in full plain sentences.
- Every number is the source's own. If it is rounded, say "about" or "nearly". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- The story is made up to show the idea. Do not claim the study happened exactly this way.
- End with SOURCES: the researchers, the title and the year, and one line on what was found. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.
- The advice in step 6 is true, safe and something a person can really do.

## 8. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

Read each line by itself, out loud, as someone who has never seen the channel:

1. Do I know who is talking, and who or what they are talking about?
2. Is it a whole sentence, in words I use every day?
3. Does this line move the story or explain it? If not, cut it.
4. After step 2, could I tell a friend the rule of the game in one sentence?
5. After step 3, do I know why each person did what they did?
6. After step 5, could I tell a friend what the real study found?
7. Does the last line answer the first?

If any answer is no, rewrite that line more plainly.

## 9. A finished example

This is the standard. Notice that every line could be understood alone, and that the characters carry the story.

```
TITLE: Why the Man Who Drank Water Paid for the Lobster
THE STRANGE ENDING IN ONE SENTENCE: Four friends split a restaurant bill equally, and the one who only drank water paid forty five dollars.
THE REAL RESULT: In a 2004 restaurant experiment, groups who split the bill equally ordered more than groups who each paid for their own.
WHO IS IN IT: the Copycat, the Cheat, the Saint, You (silent), a waiter
THE PLACE: a restaurant at night
NEEDS DRAWING: nothing

1. THE STRANGE ENDING
(A restaurant at night. A long bill lies across the table. You sits behind one glass of water.)
TRAINER: Why did the man who only drank water pay forty five *dollars?* [BIG WORDS]
TRAINER: Nobody cheated him. That is the *worst* part.

2. REWIND
(Earlier. Four friends sit down with their menus.)
TRAINER: Two hours earlier, these four friends sat down to eat.
COPYCAT: Let's just split the bill four ways. It's *easier.*
TRAINER: So they agreed on one rule. Whatever anyone orders, everyone pays a *quarter* of it.
SAINT: That sounds fair. I'll just have a small *salad.*

3. THE TRAP CLOSES
(The Cheat looks at the menu. A slow smile.)
CHEAT (thinks): The lobster costs sixty dollars. Split four ways, it only costs me *fifteen.*
CHEAT: The lobster, please. The *big* one. [REACTION: greed]
TRAINER: The other three will now pay forty five dollars of his dinner.
COPYCAT (thinks): If I'm paying for his lobster, I want one too.
COPYCAT: Make that two *lobsters.*
(The Saint looks at her salad. She closes her menu.)
SAINT: Three lobsters. I'm not paying for yours and eating *leaves.*
(The waiter turns to You. You points at his glass of water.)

4. THE FREEZE
TRAINER: Three lobsters and one glass of water. What does each person *pay?* [PAUSE]
(Silence.)

5. THE REVEAL
(The bill lands on the table and unrolls across the floor.)
TRAINER: The bill is one hundred and eighty dollars. Split four ways, that is forty five *each.* [NUMBER: $45]
TRAINER: The man who drank water pays for lobsters he never *touched.*
TRAINER: This is not a story. In 2004, three economists tested this in a real *restaurant.* [NUMBER: 2004]
TRAINER: Groups who split the bill equally ordered more food than groups who each paid for their *own.*
TRAINER: And afterwards, the diners said they would rather have paid for their own.

6. THE STING
TRAINER: You do this too. Shared taxis, group gifts, office pizza: when the cost is shared, everyone takes a little *more.* [EVERYONE]
OUTSIDE VOICE: Here is how you beat it. Say "separate bills" before anyone opens the menu. [CARD: Splitting the bill? / Say it first: / "Separate bills."]
TRAINER: So why did the water drinker pay forty five dollars? Because he trusted everyone to order like *him.*

SOURCES:
- Uri Gneezy, Ernan Haruvy and Hadas Yafe, "The Inefficiency of Splitting the Bill", The Economic Journal, 2004. In a restaurant experiment, diners who split the bill equally ordered more than diners who paid for their own, and the diners said they preferred paying for their own.
```

## 10. The layout to deliver

Use the same layout as the example: the six headed steps, each line starting with who speaks (TRAINER, SAINT, COPYCAT, CHEAT, OUTSIDE VOICE, or an extra such as WAITER), actions in round brackets on their own lines, and SOURCES at the end. Then add:

- **YOUTUBE:** a title of the form "Why..." or "How...", two plain lines of description, one line naming the real source, three to five hashtags.
- **FACEBOOK CAPTION:** one or two plain lines ending on the question the Trainer asks the viewer.
