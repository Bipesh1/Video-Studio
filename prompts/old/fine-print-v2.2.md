# What's the Catch?: how to write a script

## Script guide, explainer edition (v2.2)

For Biku: paste everything under the line into whichever AI writes the script, and fill in the INPUTS at the bottom. This edition replaces v1.0: What's the Catch? is no longer told with stick figures. It is an illustrated explainer in its own flat style (look 4, which you picked), where every explanation builds up on screen one step at a time as the narrator says it. The script now says, line by line, which picture explains that line, and names each piece in the words, so each piece appears exactly when it is said.

---

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who knows nothing about business or money, must understand every sentence the first time they hear it, and see it explained on screen at the same moment.**

If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly. No business words ("margin", "loss leader", "price discrimination", "overbooking model") unless the same sentence says what it means in everyday words.

## 2. What the video is

A two-minute vertical explainer that answers one question about something everyone has paid for or seen: why cinema popcorn costs so much, why airlines sell more seats than the plane has, why the printer was cheap and the ink is not.

- **One narrator** tells it, like a friend who works behind the counter and is letting you in on it: curious, a little amused, never smug, never angry at anyone. Nobody else speaks.
- **Every line has one picture.** The picture is a flat illustration or a diagram (section 5), and it builds up piece by piece while the line is spoken. There are no characters and no faces; when people are needed, they are simple faceless figures in a queue.
- **One explanation at a time, in order.** The viewer never sees a number before it is said, or a box before it is named.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Never a fragment such as "Popcorn. Ninety percent profit."
2. **One step to a line.** Each line explains one thing, and that one thing is what its picture shows. If a line needs two pictures, it is two lines.
3. **Name the first piece in the first few words**, so the picture never waits empty while the line gets going: "The studio can take more than half of every ticket", not "In the first weeks of a new film, the studio...".
4. **Name every piece in the words.** If the picture shows a box "THE CINEMA", the line says "the cinema". If a bar is "$7.65", the line says "seven dollars and sixty five cents". This is how each piece knows when to appear.
5. **Every number says what it is a number of.** "Of every nine dollars, the cinema keeps about seven sixty five", not "an 85 percent margin".
6. **Say numbers the way people say them**, and write them that way in the spoken line: "nine dollars", "about thirty five cents", "more than half". The picture shows them as figures ($9, 35¢).
7. **One idea to a sentence,** up to about 18 words. A line takes about 2 to 6 seconds to say.
8. **Every "it", "they" and "this" has an owner the viewer has just heard.**
9. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each step is caused by the one before.
10. No em dashes. No emoji. Mark one important word a line with asterisks for the captions: `The cinema keeps the *rest*.`

## 4. The six steps, in this order

About 260 to 300 spoken words in all. That is about two minutes.

| Step                | About      | What the viewer must come away knowing                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. The question     | 10 seconds | One question about something they have paid for or seen, 12 words or fewer, as the first line, over the place. Then one line that promises a surprise. |
| 2. The moment       | 20 seconds | The place, the price, and what everyone assumes about it.                                                                                              |
| 3. Follow the money | 35 seconds | Where each part of the money really goes, step by step.                                                                                                |
| 4. The trick        | 30 seconds | Why the business does it this way: the clever part that answers the question.                                                                          |
| 5. The proof        | 15 seconds | The real numbers with where they come from, and one other place the viewer meets the same trick.                                                       |
| 6. Your move        | 10 seconds | One thing the viewer can actually do, then one line that answers the opening question.                                                                 |

The first line must be a real question that the last line answers. It must not start with "In this video", "Did you know", "Have you ever wondered" or "Imagine".

## 5. The pictures: one after each line

After each spoken line, on its own line, write the picture in square brackets. Use only these. Every piece marked `(on: word)` appears when that word is spoken; choose a word that is in the line. Pieces without `(on: ...)` appear one after another.

- `[SCENE: cinema counter | sign: POPCORN $9 | show: machine (on: cinema), bucket (on: popcorn), hand with $10 (on: nine)]`
  An illustrated place. Only the cinema counter is drawn so far; any other place (an airport gate, a casino floor, a petrol station, a shop shelf) goes under NEEDS DRAWING with one line on what it must show.
- `[NUMBER: $9 | caption: FOR ONE BUCKET | picture: bucket | count up | on: paid]`
  One big number, counting up from nothing if you write "count up", with up to 28 letters under it. Give it a picture of what it counts whenever one fits (ticket, coin or bucket), so the page is never a bare number.
- `[RECEIPT: title: WHAT GOES IN | CORN 35¢ (on: corn) | BUCKET 45¢ (on: bucket) | BUTTER 55¢ (on: butter) | TOTAL $1.35]`
  A receipt printing line by line, up to six lines and a total in red.
- `[SPLIT: title: WHERE YOUR / $9 GOES | THE CINEMA $7.65 85% (on: cinema) | BUTTER 55¢ 6% | BUCKET 45¢ 5% | CORN 35¢ 4%]`
  One bar split into shares, filling share by share. Two to five shares, their percentages adding up to 100.
- `[FLOW: title: FOLLOW THE / MONEY | YOU (on: your) -> $15 TICKET / $9 POPCORN (on: ticket) -> THE CINEMA (on: goes) -> MOST OF THE TICKET, aside KEEPS THE POPCORN (on: mostly) -> THE FILM STUDIO (on: studio)]`
  Two to four boxes from top to bottom, an arrow between each two with coins flowing down it, a label beside each arrow and, if you want, a second label on the other side.
- `[BARS: title: WHAT THE CINEMA / KEEPS | ONE BUCKET 7.65 "$7.65" (on: bucket) | ONE TICKET 6 "$6" (on: ticket) | red: ONE BUCKET]`
  Two to five bars growing, their numbers counting up; one in red, the one that matters.
- `[TAG: title: SHARE IT | $4.50 | note: EACH, SHARED | was $9 (on: costs) | on: share]`
  A price tag dropping in and swinging, with up to 18 letters on it, and an old price crossed out.
- `[QUEUE: title: EVERY ONE OF US / PAYS $9 | 4 people | sign: $9 | on: every]`
  Faceless people walking into a queue at a counter, under a price sign.

Every picture may have a title: one or two short lines at the top (a "/" splits them), up to 24 letters each; the line in red is the one that matters. Box and bar labels are up to 28 letters; a "/" inside a label breaks it in two.

**Make it attractive:** when the same thing is in two pictures one after the other (the bucket at the counter, then the bucket with its price), say so in the second marker with `(from: bucket)`: the camera turns the one into the other. Change the kind of picture from line to line, so something new arrives every two or three seconds; use the SPLIT or FLOW once each for the big reveal; save the red for the one number that answers the question; end on a TAG or a NUMBER the viewer can act on.

Do not write: characters, faces or dialogue; a picture that is not in the list; more than one picture for one line; real logos or brand names in the pictures; a real company accused of anything a source does not say.

## 6. The facts

- Build the video on real, checkable numbers: a price, a share, a margin, a count. Every number is the source's own. If it is rounded, say "about" or "nearly". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- A number that changes by country or year says which ("in the US in 2025").
- Do not claim a named company does something unless a source says so.
- End with SOURCES: who published it, the title and the year, and one line on what it says. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.
- The tip in step 6 is legal, safe and something a person can really do.

## 7. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

1. Do I know what or whom every line is about?
2. Is it a whole sentence?
3. Is there any business word I would have to already know?
4. Is every piece of the picture named in its line's words?
5. After step 3, could I tell a friend where the money goes?
6. Does the last line answer the first?

If any answer is no, rewrite that line more plainly.

## 8. A finished example (the opening)

```
1. THE QUESTION
NARRATOR: Why does a bucket of cinema popcorn cost nine dollars?
[SCENE: cinema counter | title: WHY DOES POPCORN / COST $9? | show: machine (on: cinema), bucket (on: popcorn), hand with $10 (on: nine)]
NARRATOR: Almost everyone has paid it, and almost nobody asks why.
[NUMBER: $9 | caption: FOR ONE BUCKET | count up | on: paid]

2. THE MOMENT
NARRATOR: The corn costs about thirty five cents, the bucket forty five, and the butter fifty five.
[RECEIPT: title: WHAT GOES IN | CORN 35¢ (on: corn) | BUCKET 45¢ (on: bucket) | BUTTER 55¢ (on: butter) | TOTAL $1.35]

3. FOLLOW THE MONEY
NARRATOR: So the cinema keeps the *rest*, about seven dollars and sixty five cents.
[SPLIT: title: WHERE YOUR / $9 GOES | THE CINEMA $7.65 85% (on: cinema) | BUTTER 55¢ 6% (on: rest) | BUCKET 45¢ 5% | CORN 35¢ 4%]
```

(The numbers in this example are samples to show the layout; a real script takes them from its sources.)
