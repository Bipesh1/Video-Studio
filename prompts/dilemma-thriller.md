# The Dilemma Table thrillers: how to write a script

## Script guide, thriller edition (v1.0)

For Biku: paste everything under the line into whichever AI writes the script, and fill in the INPUTS at the bottom. This is the thriller and mystery series of the game theory channel: two-minute films told from inside the danger, where the twist is a real result of game theory. It is built from the plain-words guide (`prompts/dilemma.md`, v2.3) and changes what a thriller needs: the story starts in the danger, the viewer guesses, the lesson arrives as the twist. Danger is real but nothing violent is shown: that is a choice for you to keep or change.

---

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who has never seen this channel and knows nothing about game theory, must understand every sentence the first time they hear it.**

A thriller tempts you to be vague and moody. Do not. The tension comes from what is clear: who is in danger, what the rule is, how little time is left. If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly.

## 2. What the video is

A two-minute vertical cartoon thriller. A few people are trapped in a game with deadly or high stakes: a ship in a storm, a locked vault, a prison cell, a stuck lift, a standoff in the street. Each must choose without knowing what the others will do. The ending is a twist the viewer did not expect, and the twist is the real answer game theory gives. Then, briefly, the viewer learns it was a real puzzle or study, and where they meet the same thing in their own life.

- **One place, one night, told in order.** Name it in one plain line at the start ("A pirate ship, in the middle of a storm."). No flashbacks, no second location, no dream.
- **The Trainer tells it.** He is a calm man in a dark suit and a gold tie. He is heard over the story like a storyteller who already knows how it ends, in full plain sentences. He is seen only at the very end, in his dark room with one bare bulb, where he says it was real and answers the opening question.
- **The players** are the channel's regulars. A script uses **two of the three**, and one of them is the person we follow:
  - **The Saint** (she; glasses, blue scarf): trusts people, wants it fair.
  - **The Copycat** (he; orange beanie): does what the others do, frightened of being the odd one out.
  - **The Cheat** (he; red striped tie): takes what he can, and is often the one who sees the trick first.
    They keep their own clothes in every story. The place and the props tell us we are on a ship or in a prison, not costumes.
- **Other people**, if the game needs them: plain grey figures. At most four people in a picture; a crowd of five or more makes every face too small to read on a phone.
- **A voice from outside** reads one piece of advice near the end. It is a different voice from everyone in the story.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Every line has someone doing something. Never a fragment such as "Storm. Midnight. Five pirates."
2. **Say what is happening.** The Trainer tells the viewer each step: who these people are, what they want, the rule they cannot break, what just changed, and why it matters.
3. **No insider words.** No "Nash", "equilibrium", "dominant strategy", "backward induction", "truel", "prisoner's dilemma". Say the idea in everyday words: "think about what happens if they say no".
4. **Every "he", "she", "they" and "it" has an owner the viewer has just heard.**
5. **Every number says what it is a number of.** "Ninety-eight of the hundred coins", not "98 percent".
6. **One idea to a sentence,** up to about 18 words. Two short sentences can share a line.
7. **People talk the way frightened people talk,** in short whole sentences: "Ninety-eight? You'll be swimming by morning."
8. **Thoughts** are written `SAINT (thinks): One coin, or nothing.` They are heard while the mouth stays shut. Use three or four, at the moments someone decides.
9. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each thing that happens is caused by the one before.
10. **Questions are short and whole.** A question to the viewer is one plain sentence that ends with its question mark, never broken in the middle.
11. No em dashes. No emoji. Mark one or two important words a line with asterisks for the captions: `He keeps *ninety-eight* coins.`

## 3b. Make it a thriller: the viewer is in the room with them

1. **Start in the danger.** The first picture is already tense: the storm, the locked door, the smoke. No calm introduction.
2. **A clock.** Something makes waiting impossible and gets worse through the film: the water rising, the guards' footsteps, the candle burning down, the vote about to close. Show it at least three times.
3. **Fair clues.** Everything the viewer needs to guess the twist is shown before it. The twist surprises, but on a second watch it was obvious.
4. **Let the viewer guess.** Once, before the twist, the Trainer asks the viewer what they think happens next. Write `(Silence.)` after it. The studio holds the picture there.
5. **Two cliffhangers.** The film is shown in parts of under a minute. End the first third and the second third on an open question or a sudden change, and mark each line `[CLIFFHANGER]`.
6. **Write what we see, often.** An action line in round brackets before most spoken lines: who moves, who looks at whom, what changes. About one action line for every two spoken lines.
7. **The sound of the place,** in round brackets when it first matters: `(Thunder. The lamp swings.)` The studio has a storm's wind and rain, a heartbeat that speeds up, a clock ticking, footsteps, a door creak, a boom for the moment everything turns, and dark music that tightens. `(The music stops.)` drops everything to silence for a moment.
8. **One person to follow.** The viewer stays with the one the game closes on; we see the others through their eyes.
9. **Danger, never gore.** People can be in mortal danger and lose. Nobody is shown hurt: the camera cuts away, the light goes out, or the Trainer says what happened. No weapon is ever fired at a person on screen.

## 4. The six steps, in this order

About 260 to 290 spoken words in all. That is two minutes.

| Step            | About      | What the viewer must come away knowing                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. The question | 5 seconds  | One question to the viewer, 12 words or fewer, about "you", as the first line, shown as big words over the dangerous place. Then one short line promising an answer.                                               |
| 2. The rules    | 20 seconds | Where we are, who is here, what each wants, and the one rule nobody can break. The clock starts.                                                                                                                   |
| 3. The squeeze  | 40 seconds | Each choice makes it worse: an offer, a lie, a threat, a betrayal. Thoughts at the decisions. The first `[CLIFFHANGER]` falls here.                                                                                |
| 4. The twist    | 25 seconds | The Trainer asks the viewer what happens next, `(Silence.)`, then the surprising result, shown happening, and in one or two plain sentences why it had to happen. The second `[CLIFFHANGER]` comes just before it. |
| 5. It was real  | 15 seconds | In the dark room: "This is a real puzzle" (or study), who found it and the year, and one everyday place the viewer meets the same thing.                                                                           |
| 6. T            |
| he answer       | 15 seconds | The outside voice gives one thing a person can actually do. Then the Trainer answers the opening question in one line.                                                                                             |

The first line must be a real question to the viewer that the last line answers. It must not start with "In this video", "Did you know", "Have you ever wondered" or "Imagine".

## 5. What the studio can show

You do not write camera directions. Write the words, and the studio chooses the pictures. You may ask for these, in square brackets after a line:

- `[BIG WORDS]` the line itself large on the screen. The first line always has it.
- `[NUMBER: 98 of 100]` one large number, with a few words under it.
- `[CARD: up to four short lines]` a card on the screen, for the advice near the end.
- `[WHAT YOU GET: label / label]` two outcomes shown as pictures, one above the other, each with a short label. Once, where the result is told.
- `[EVERYONE]` the camera starts on one person and pulls back to show a whole crowd doing the same thing. Once, on the line that says "you do this too".
- `[REACTION: greed]` or `[REACTION: shock]` one big cartoon reaction (dollar signs in the eyes, or lines bursting round the head) on the one moment the story turns. Once a film at most.
- `[CLIFFHANGER]` the end of a part (see 3b).

Actions on their own line in round brackets, in plain words: `(The captain pushes the gold to his own side.)` `(She raises her hand, slowly.)`

People can sit, stand, walk in or out, reach across a table, point, shrug, cross their arms, slump, put a hand on their heart, raise their hands, wave, and look afraid, terrified, suspicious, calculating, tempted, lying, guilty, beaten or empty. Things already drawn: gold coins, cash, banknotes, an envelope, a phone, a cup, a bag, bread, a wall clock, smoke that fills a room, fire, a bulb that flickers and fails. A new place or thing the story needs (a ship's cabin, a prison cell, a lift) is drawn for the first script that needs it: list it under NEEDS DRAWING, one line each, at most three.

Do not write: a second place, a flashback, more than four people in one picture, a crowd that comes forward, a fight, anyone shown hurt, a weapon fired at a person on screen, real living people as characters, or any real brand. Do not have anyone pass a small thing from hand to hand, or pick a card up: the studio cannot show those yet.

## 6. The facts

- Build the film on one real result: a famous puzzle, a game, or a study. Its twist is that result, and it must be right. Work it out step by step before you write, and check it against the source.
- In step 5 say it was real, who published it and the year, in a full plain sentence. Every number is the source's own; if it is rounded, say "about" or "nearly". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- The story is made up to show the idea. Do not claim the people or the event were real unless they were.
- End with SOURCES: the author, the title, where it was published and the year, and one line on what it shows. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.
- The advice in step 6 is true, safe and something a person can really do.

## 7. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

Read each line by itself, out loud, as someone who has never seen the channel:

1. Do I know who is talking, and who or what they are talking about?
2. Is it a whole sentence?
3. Is there any word I would have to already know the show, or maths, to understand?
4. After step 2, could I tell a friend the rule of the game and what each person wants?
5. After step 4, could I explain the twist to a friend in one sentence?
6. Was every clue to the twist shown before it?
7. Does the last line answer the first?

If any answer is no, rewrite that line more plainly.

## 8. A finished example

This is the standard. Notice that every line could be understood alone, the clock keeps running, and the twist was set up in the rules.

```
TITLE: How to Take Almost Everything and Still Win the Vote
THE TWIST IN ONE SENTENCE: A leader who offers a tiny reward to the people with the worst alternative can keep almost everything, because one coin beats nothing.
WHO WE FOLLOW: the Saint. The Cheat is the captain. The Copycat and two plain pirates are the crew.
THE PLACE: a pirate ship's cabin in a storm.
IN THE SCENE: a chest of gold coins, a lamp swinging from the ceiling.
NEEDS DRAWING: a ship's cabin (wooden walls, a round window with rain on it); a chest of gold; a swinging oil lamp.

1. THE QUESTION
(A storm. A ship's cabin lit by one swinging lamp. Five pirates round a chest of gold.)
TRAINER: If you split a fortune five ways, how much could you keep? [BIG WORDS]
TRAINER: Almost all of it. Watch how.

2. THE RULES
TRAINER: Five pirates have one chest of gold, a hundred coins. Their captain is this man in the red tie.
TRAINER: The rule is old and simple. The captain says how to split the gold, and everyone votes.
TRAINER: If at least half say yes, it is done. If not, the captain goes over the side, and the next pirate tries.
(Thunder. Water drips through the ceiling.)
SAINT (thinks): Five of us, a hundred coins. Twenty each is the only fair way.

3. THE SQUEEZE
(The captain smiles and pushes the gold to his own side.) [REACTION: greed]
CHEAT: Ninety-eight coins for me. One for you, and one for him. Nothing for the other two.
COPYCAT: Ninety-eight? You'll be swimming with the sharks by morning. [CLIFFHANGER]
(Everyone turns to look at the two who were offered one coin. The Saint is one of them.)
TRAINER: One coin is almost nothing. But think about what she gets if she says no.

4. THE TWIST
TRAINER: If the captain goes over, four are left. The next man needs only one more vote, and he buys it with one coin.
TRAINER: But not from these two. If the captain goes, they get *nothing*.
TRAINER: So what do you think she does? [CLIFFHANGER]
(Silence.)
(The Saint looks at her one coin. Slowly, she raises her hand. The other one raises his.)
SAINT (thinks): One coin, or nothing.
TRAINER: Two votes, and his own makes three out of five. He keeps *ninety-eight* coins.

5. IT WAS REAL
(The dark room. One bare bulb. The Trainer at his table.)
TRAINER: This is a real puzzle. The mathematician Ian Stewart wrote about it in Scientific American in 1999. [NUMBER: 98 of 100]
TRAINER: It works on anyone who compares an offer with what they get if they refuse.
TRAINER: You see it every time someone offers you a tiny raise, because they know you have nowhere else to go.

6. THE ANSWER
OUTSIDE VOICE: Before you take a small offer, find out what else you could get. [CARD: Small offer? / Find another option. / Then say no.]
TRAINER: So how much could you keep? Everything they cannot afford to *refuse*.

SOURCES:
- Ian Stewart, "A Puzzle for Pirates", Mathematical Recreations, Scientific American, May 1999. Five logical pirates split 100 coins by vote; the most senior keeps 98 and gives one coin each to the two pirates who would get nothing if he were thrown overboard.
```

## 9. The layout to deliver

Use the same layout as the example: the six headed steps, each line starting with who speaks (TRAINER, SAINT, COPYCAT, CHEAT or OUTSIDE VOICE), actions in round brackets on their own lines, two `[CLIFFHANGER]` marks, and SOURCES at the end. Then add:

- **YOUTUBE:** a title of the form "How to..." or a question, two plain lines of description that do not give the twist away, one line naming the source, three to five hashtags.
- **FACEBOOK CAPTION:** one or two plain lines ending on the question the Trainer asks the viewer, without the answer.
