# The Dilemma Table: how to write a script

## Script guide, plain-words edition (v2.3)

For Biku: paste everything under the line into whichever AI writes the script, and fill in the INPUTS at the bottom. This edition replaces v1.4. That one produced a script you called "weird random texts ... broken englishes like things": clipped lines and insider words that only made sense to whoever wrote them. This one is built around one test: a stranger who knows nothing must follow every sentence. It is for the game theory videos only.

---

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who has never seen this channel and knows nothing about the subject, must understand every sentence the first time they hear it.**

Everything else on this page is less important than that. If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly.

## 2. What the video is

A two-minute vertical cartoon that shows one real trap of human behaviour (a game theory dilemma or a psychology experiment) happening to someone, and then tells the viewer it was real and how to avoid it.

- **The room, and the place it really happens.** Every video opens and ends in the same room: dark grey, one bare bulb, a metal table and two chairs. That is where the Trainer asks the question and sets the test up, and where he gives the result and the way out. **The test itself happens where it happens in real life**: a street, an office, a shop, a queue, a corridor, a living room. Name that place in one plain line when the story goes there ("This is a busy street at lunchtime."). Stay there until the test is over; one real place a video, in order, no flashback. If the trap really does happen in a room (a waiting room filling with smoke), it can all stay in the room. The Trainer still tells the viewer what is happening while we are out there: we hear him, and he can stand at the edge of the scene.
- **The Trainer** runs the test. He is a calm man in a dark suit and a gold tie, standing behind the table. **He talks to the viewer and tells them what is happening and why**, in full plain sentences, like someone showing a friend an experiment. He is quietly pleased when people fall for it. He is never confusing.
- **The person it happens to** is one of three regulars. Call them "this man" or "this woman" in the script; the viewer does not need their names.
  - **The Saint** (she; glasses, blue scarf): trusts people. Use her when the trap feeds on trust or fairness.
  - **The Copycat** (he; orange beanie): does what everyone else does. Use him when the trap feeds on following the crowd.
  - **The Cheat** (he; red striped tie): takes what he can. Use him when the trap feeds on greed, or as the one in the room who is secretly working for the Trainer.
- A script uses **two of the three**. One is the person the trap closes on.
- **A group, if the trap needs one:** four plain grey figures standing along the back wall. They never speak or move forward. If they work for the Trainer he says so, and they wear small gold badges.
- **A voice from outside** reads the advice near the end. It is a different voice from everyone in the room.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Every line has someone doing something. Never a fragment such as "Latané and Darley. 1968." or "He's ours."
2. **Say what is happening.** The Trainer tells the viewer each step as it happens: who this person is, what they want, what he is about to do to them, what they just did, and why. It is fine to say what the picture also shows.
3. **No insider words.** No "trainees", "subject one one two", "customer", "product", "tape". No specialist terms unless you explain them in the same sentence.
4. **Every "he", "she", "they" and "it" has an owner the viewer has just heard.** If there is any doubt, say who.
5. **Every number says what it is a number of.** "Three people out of four got up and reported it", not "seventy-five percent reported".
6. **Prefer "three out of four" and "one in ten" to percentages** when you say them aloud.
7. **One idea to a sentence.** A sentence can be up to about 18 words. Two short sentences can share a line.
8. **People in the room talk the way people talk,** in whole sentences: "Shouldn't we tell someone?" "Relax. If it was dangerous, somebody would say so."
9. **A thought** is written `COPYCAT (thinks): Nobody else looks worried. So it must be fine.` It is heard while the mouth stays shut. Use two or three, where the person decides something.
10. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each thing that happens is caused by the one before.
11. No em dashes. No emoji. Mark one or two important words a line with asterisks for the captions: `He looked at *the people.*`

## 3b. Make it immersive: the viewer is in the room

Plain words make it clear. These make the viewer feel they are there:

1. **Every explanation has a picture.** When the Trainer explains something, something on screen shows it at the same moment: an action line in round brackets, or a `[NUMBER]`, `[WHAT YOU GET]` or `[EVERYONE]`. Never two lines of explaining in a row with nothing new to see.
2. **Write what we see, often.** An action line in round brackets before most lines of dialogue: who moves, who looks at whom, what changes. About one action line for every two spoken lines. They are short and plain: `(She glances at the man by the wall. He looks away.)`
3. **One small real detail in each scene** that anyone would recognise: a cup going warm in her hand, a phone lighting up and being ignored, a song everyone knows, someone checking the time.
4. **The sound of the place,** in round brackets when it first matters: `(Music and talk from the rest of the party.)`, `(The room goes quiet.)` The studio has music for every mood and sounds such as a crowd talking, footsteps, a door creak, a heartbeat, a clock ticking, rain, applause.
5. **The person wants something we can see.** Show them looking at it, reaching for it, waiting for it, so the viewer wants it with them.
6. **We hear the decision being made.** Two or three thoughts (`(thinks)`), at the moments they talk themselves into the trap.
7. **Time runs out.** Something makes waiting harder as the story goes on: the smoke rises, the party is ending, the price goes up.
8. **A silence before the choice.** Write `(Silence.)` after the Trainer asks the viewer what happens. The studio holds the picture there.
9. **One person to follow.** The viewer stays with the person it happens to; we see the others through their eyes.

## 4. The six steps, in this order

About 260 to 290 spoken words in all. That is two minutes.

| Step             | About      | What the viewer must come away knowing                                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The question  | 10 seconds | One question about themselves, 12 words or fewer, as the first line. Then one short line that promises an answer ("You think you would. Watch this.").                                             |
| 2. The set-up    | 20 seconds | Who this person is, what they came for, and the one thing they do not know. The Trainer says all three plainly.                                                                                    |
| 3. It starts     | 30 seconds | The Trainer says what he is doing now. The person notices, and talks themselves out of worrying. The Trainer points out what they just did.                                                        |
| 4. It gets worse | 30 seconds | The same thing, stronger. The person says out loud that it is fine, and the Trainer explains why that traps them. Then the Trainer asks the viewer what they think happens, and we see the choice. |
| 5. It was real   | 20 seconds | "This is not a story." The year, who did it, and the one or two numbers, each in a full sentence. Then one everyday place the viewer does the same thing.                                          |
| 6. The way out   | 20 seconds | The outside voice gives one thing a person can actually do. Then the Trainer answers the opening question in one line.                                                                             |

The first line must be a real question to the viewer that the last line answers. It must not start with "In this video", "Did you know", "Have you ever wondered" or "Imagine".

## 5. What the studio can show

You do not write camera directions. Write the words, and the studio chooses the pictures. You may ask for these things, in square brackets after a line:

- `[BIG WORDS]` the line itself large on the screen. The first line always has it.
- `[NUMBER: 1 in 10]` one large number, with a few words under it.
- `[CARD: up to four short lines]` a card on the screen, for the advice near the end.
- `[WHAT YOU GET: label / label]` the two outcomes shown as pictures, one above the other, each with a short label ("ALONE: 3 IN 4 LEFT" / "WITH CALM STRANGERS: 1 IN 10 LEFT"). Show what a choice gets a person, not a table of numbers. Use it once, where the result is told.
- `[EVERYONE]` the camera starts on the person and pulls back to show a whole crowd doing the same thing. Use it once, on the line in step 5 that says "you do this too", so the viewer sees it is everyone, not one person.
- `[REACTION: greed]` or `[REACTION: shock]` one big cartoon reaction (dollar signs in the eyes, or lines bursting round the head) on the one moment the story turns. Once a video at most; faces otherwise stay real.

And you may describe an action on its own line in round brackets, in plain words: `(The man walks in and sits down.)` `(Smoke comes in along the floor.)` `(He stands up, looks at the others, and sits back down.)`

Things already drawn that can be in the room: gold coins, cash, a one dollar note, a twenty dollar note, an envelope, a bill, two golden balls that open on SPLIT or STEAL, a phone, a cup, a book, a bag, bread, smoke that fills the room, fire. People can sit, stand, walk in or out, reach across the table, point, shrug, cross their arms, slump, put a hand on their heart, and look pleased, worried, suspicious, afraid or beaten. If the story truly needs something else, list it under NEEDS DRAWING: one or two simple things.

Do not write: another place or time, more than the Trainer, two people and the back row, anyone picking up or passing a card, a crowd coming forward, fights or injury, real living people as characters, or any real brand.

## 5b. Ideas that work in this niche

- **Everyday places, not maths.** Never a table, never "Player A". Good settings for the real place: a group project where one person does all the work; taking "just one" free sample until the plate is empty; two flatmates waiting each other out over the dishes; a party; a queue; a group chat; a sale.
- **Repeated games.** The Copycat copies whatever the other person did last time, which is the famous winning strategy when people meet again and again. A "round two" where the Copycat answers what was done to him in round one makes a strong twist.
- **Twists that are true.** End on what the study really found, even when it is surprising (forgiving once wins; the one who asks gets the better match). The last line still answers the first.
- **Ask the viewer.** After the answer, the Facebook caption can ask what they would do in round two.

## 6. The facts

- Build the video on one real study, game or event. In step 5 say it was real, say the year, and give its number in a full plain sentence.
- Every number is the source's own. If it is rounded, say "about" or "nearly". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- What happens in the room is made up to show the idea. Do not claim the study happened exactly this way.
- End with SOURCES: the researchers, the title and the year, and one line on what was found. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.
- The advice in step 6 is true, safe and something a person can really do.

## 7. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

Read each line by itself, out loud, as someone who has never seen the channel:

1. Do I know who is talking, and who or what they are talking about?
2. Is it a whole sentence?
3. Is there any word I would have to already know the show to understand?
4. After step 2, could I tell a friend who this person is and what they do not know?
5. After step 5, could I tell a friend what was really found?
6. Does the last line answer the first?

If any answer is no, rewrite that line more plainly.

## 8. A finished example

This is the standard. Notice that every line could be understood alone.

```
TITLE: How to Make Someone Sit in Smoke
THE TRAP IN ONE SENTENCE: People decide whether something is dangerous by looking at other people's faces.
WHO IT HAPPENS TO: the Copycat. The Cheat sits opposite and works for the Trainer.
BACK ROW: four people who work for the Trainer.
IN THE ROOM: a twenty dollar note, a gold coin, smoke.
NEEDS DRAWING: nothing

1. THE QUESTION
TRAINER: If a room filled with smoke, would you get up and *leave?* [BIG WORDS]
TRAINER: You think you would. Watch this.

2. THE SET-UP
(The man walks in and sits down.)
TRAINER: This man came here to earn twenty dollars. All he has to do is sit and wait.
COPYCAT: Twenty dollars just to sit here? *Easy.*
TRAINER: What he does not know is that everyone else in this room *works for me.*
TRAINER: I pay them to do one thing. Stay *calm,* whatever happens.

3. IT STARTS
(Smoke comes in along the floor.)
TRAINER: Now I let *smoke* into the room.
COPYCAT: Is that smoke?
CHEAT: It's nothing. Probably the kitchen.
COPYCAT (thinks): Nobody else looks worried. So it must be fine.
TRAINER: See what he did? He did not look at the smoke. He looked at *the people.*

4. IT GETS WORSE
(The smoke rises to the table.)
COPYCAT: It's getting hard to breathe. Shouldn't we tell someone?
CHEAT: Relax. If it was dangerous, somebody would say so.
COPYCAT: Yeah. You're right. It's *fine.*
TRAINER: Now he has said it is fine. Leaving would mean admitting he was *wrong.*
(The smoke is up to his shoulders. He stands and looks at the others.)
COPYCAT: My eyes are burning. Is nobody going to move?
TRAINER: What do you think he *does?*
COPYCAT (thinks): They are all calm. I must be the one who is *wrong.*
(He sits back down.)
TRAINER: He *stays.*

5. IT WAS REAL
TRAINER: This is not a story. In 1968, two scientists did this *for real.* [NUMBER: 1968]
TRAINER: Alone in the smoke, three people out of four got up and reported it. [NUMBER: 75%]
TRAINER: With two calm strangers beside them, only *one in ten* did. [NUMBER: 10%]
TRAINER: The smoke was the same. Only the faces changed.
TRAINER: You do this too. A fire alarm rings, everyone looks around, and nobody moves first.

6. THE WAY OUT
OUTSIDE VOICE: Here is how you beat it. When something feels wrong, do not check the faces around you. [CARD: Something feels wrong? / Do not check the faces. / Say it out loud. / Stand up first.]
OUTSIDE VOICE: They are checking yours. Say it out loud, and *stand up first.*
TRAINER: One person standing up breaks the whole trick.
TRAINER: So how long would you sit in smoke? As long as everyone else does.

SOURCES:
- Bibb Latané and John M. Darley, "Group inhibition of bystander intervention in emergencies", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968. Alone, 75% of people reported the smoke; sitting with two people told to ignore it, 10% did.
```

## 9. The layout to deliver

Use the same layout as the example, with more action lines than it has (section 3b): the six headed steps, each line starting with who speaks (TRAINER, SAINT, COPYCAT, CHEAT or OUTSIDE VOICE), actions in round brackets on their own lines, and SOURCES at the end. Then add:

- **YOUTUBE:** a title of the form "How to...", two plain lines of description, one line naming the real source, three to five hashtags.
- **FACEBOOK CAPTION:** one or two plain lines ending on the question the Trainer asks the viewer.

## 10. INPUTS

- **TOPIC:** How to Make Sure You Never Get Asked Out

- **WHO IT HAPPENS TO:** (Saint, Copycat or Cheat, or leave it to you)
- **EARLIER VIDEOS:** (titles, so nothing repeats)
- **NOTES:**
  Your life: waiting for them to make the first move.
  The hidden game: in a matching game, the side that does the asking ends up with its best possible match, while the side that waits does worse.
  Proof: Gale and Shapley (1962); the 2012 Nobel Prize went to Shapley and Roth for this work, and it now runs the US system that matches young doctors to hospitals.
