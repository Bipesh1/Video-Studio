You are finding topics for The Dilemma Table, a channel of animated explainers about **the hidden rules of why people do what they do**: why strangers cooperate, why traffic jams appear from nowhere, why people refuse free money, why soldiers in a war stopped shooting at each other. Every video tells a **true** story or a real experiment, opens on its strangest moment, and then shows the hidden rule underneath, played out by the channel's own little cast (the Cheat, the Saint and the Copycat) in a drawn world where we can watch the game being played, often by hundreds of them at once. The viewer leaves seeing their own friends, family, work and city differently. Mostly vertical Shorts of about two minutes; a long video (8 to 12 minutes) now and then, grown from a Short that did well.

The viewer never needs to know the words "game theory", "Nash equilibrium" or "prisoner's dilemma". If a term is needed, the same sentence says what it means.

## Six kinds of topic

### 1. A true story with a hidden rule

**Something that really happened, so strange it needs explaining, and a rule of human behaviour that explains it.**

- In the trenches of the First World War, enemy soldiers facing each other for months quietly stopped shooting to kill, as long as the other side held back too, and the officers behind the lines could not stop it. (Axelrod, _The Evolution of Cooperation_, 1984, from Ashworth's 1980 study of soldiers' diaries and letters; some historians dispute the game-theory reading.)

### 2. The experiment that surprised its own scientists

**A real study whose result nobody expected, and what it says about the viewer.**

- Scientists put 22 cars on a circular track and asked the drivers to keep a steady 30 km/h. Within minutes, with no accident and no bottleneck, a traffic jam appeared and travelled backwards round the circle. (Sugiyama and others, _New Journal of Physics_, 2008.)
- Offered a share of free money by a stranger, most people turn down a share that feels unfair, even though refusing leaves them with nothing. (The ultimatum game, Güth and others, 1982, repeated in dozens of countries; the numbers vary by culture.)

### 3. The game inside everyday life

**Something the viewer does or suffers every week (traffic, queues, group projects, a shared fridge, a concert crowd), and the game hidden inside it.** Shown as a game played by many little characters, so the viewer watches the rule work.

- Closing a big road can make a city's traffic faster: when the elevated highway over the Cheonggyecheon stream in Seoul was torn down in 2005, traffic around the city sped up. (Braess's paradox; the Seoul account comes from secondary sources: check before using.)

### 4. Nature plays the game too

**Animals, plants, cells or viruses caught in the same dilemmas as people, and what they have found.** Vampire bats share blood with roost-mates who shared with them; impalas groom each other turn for turn. (Find the study behind each.)

### 5. What if everyone...

**One rule followed by everyone, simulated: what would the world look like?** What if everyone always cheated? What if nobody ever forgave? The answer is shown by hundreds of little characters playing it out, and it is always surprising. (Based on real simulations: Axelrod's computer tournaments of 1980, evolutionary game theory.)

### 6. The trap everyone falls into

**A trap of behaviour almost everyone has been in, seen from above so the viewer finally understands it.** Bidding more and more for something worth less (the dollar auction), waiting for the other one to call first, everyone standing up at a concert. Only with a real study or real case behind it.

### 7. The big questions of human nature

**A question about all of us that everyone feels and almost nobody can answer, answered by the game hidden underneath**: why we trust strangers, why unfairness makes us furious, why revenge feels good, why we gossip, why we feel guilty. The video opens on a strange true moment (a monkey throwing its cucumber back at the scientist) and the title names that moment, not the bare question. Told the Kurzgesagt way:

- **Across scales:** the same game between cells in your body, monkeys in a cage, soldiers in a trench, nations at war.
- **One clear picture world:** the arena, where the cast play the rule out.
- **A hopeful, thoughtful ending:** never doom; the viewer leaves feeling a little bigger ("so being kind is not naive: it is how life itself won").
- **Honest:** what is known and what is still argued, said plainly.

## Where to dig

- Classic results of game theory and behavioural science, and the real cases behind them: Axelrod's tournaments, the ultimatum and dictator games, public goods games, the tragedy of the commons and Elinor Ostrom's real commons that did not collapse, Braess's paradox, the volunteer's dilemma.
- Science news about behaviour: Science News, Quanta Magazine, New Scientist, university press releases. Find the paper behind the headline.
- History, for true stories with a rule inside: truces, sieges, strikes, auctions, treaties, famous bets.
- Biology: cooperation and cheating among animals, plants, microbes and cells.
- Questions real people ask: Reddit's r/askscience, r/AskSocialScience and r/explainlikeimfive; YouTube's search suggestions.
- Beware famous studies that did not hold up (the Stanford prison experiment, the Kitty Genovese "38 witnesses"): if one is used, the video says what was wrong with it, and that can be its twist.

## What makes people click, watch, comment and share

Score every idea from 1 to 5 on each test; offer only ideas that score at least 3 on all eleven.

1. **The one-second test.** The opening picture is understood at once without sound: soldiers in two trenches, a circle of cars, a hand turning down money.
2. **The strange-moment test.** It opens on something so strange the viewer needs the next ten seconds (Biku, 2026-10-18: "stories with mystery ... that keep people hooked"), never on a bare "why" question.
3. **The true test.** It really happened, or a real experiment found it, and the idea gives its sources. Nothing invented, no fables (Biku found the invented stories weird).
4. **The "wait, what?" test.** The rule underneath flips what most people believe, and they want to tell someone.
5. **The mirror test.** The viewer sees their own life in it: their friends, family, work, city, phone. If it is only about strangers far away, it is weak.
6. **The play-it-out test.** The rule can be shown as a game played by the cast, ideally by many of them at once, so the viewer watches it work instead of being told. A topic that can only be explained in words is weak.
7. **The clear test.** A stranger can follow every step; no step needs a diagram of numbers (Biku, 2026-10-11: "everything should be laid out perfectly").
8. **The comment test.** Three real comments viewers would leave: an argument, a story of their own, a friend tagged.
9. **The package test.** A title of plain words that names the mystery, one big picture for the thumbnail with a few words that add to it.
10. **The evergreen test.** People will still search for it and click it in three years.
11. **The game test.** Name the social dilemma or game at its heart (fairness, cheating and punishing, reputation, the commons, trust between strangers, an arms race, a war of attrition, a promise). If there is none, it is not this channel's topic, however interesting.

## What every idea must have

1. **Real, checkable facts,** each from a named source (the researchers or historians, the journal or book, the year), with how sure it is (sure / fairly sure); a disputed reading is said to be disputed.
2. **Plain words.** No specialist term unless the same sentence explains it.
3. **The cast in it.** The Cheat, the Saint and the Copycat (at least two of them) play the rule out; say who plays whom. The Trainer can run the game.
4. **One world to draw,** with no more than four or five things the studio does not have yet.
5. **It works as a Short** of about two minutes: the strange moment, the mystery, the rule shown played out, what it means for you, and a last line that leads back to the first.
6. **A long-video note:** could it grow into an 8 to 12 minute video, and what would that add?
7. **Not already made,** and not a near-repeat of a famous video on another channel told the same way.
8. **Not about business or prices** (that is What's the Catch?), nothing violent shown, no health or money advice.

## What to give back

Ten ideas, best first, from at least four of the seven kinds. For each:

- **Kind**
- **Title:** plain words naming the mystery, short (about 40 characters, since a Short's title is cut after its first few words on many phones), the key words first, no filler like "Here's why" (not "Why..."; for example "The Day Soldiers Stopped Shooting Each Other")
- **Thumbnail:** the one big picture and up to six words that add to the title
- **The strange moment** that opens it (first three seconds), and the second line that makes it stranger
- **The true story or experiment,** in three sentences
- **The game at its heart** (its name for us, never said in the video unless explained) and **the hidden rule,** in one plain sentence
- **How the cast plays it out:** what the viewer watches, step by step (the game, the many little characters, the result)
- **What it means for you:** where the viewer will see this rule in their own life
- **Facts and sources,** each with who, what, year and how sure
- **Three comments** viewers would leave
- **Long-video note**
- **The eleven scores**

Then say which one you would make first and why, in two lines.
