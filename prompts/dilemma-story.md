# The Dilemma Table: how to write a script

## Script guide, story edition (v4.0)

For Biku: paste everything under the line into whichever AI writes the script, and fill in the INPUTS at the bottom.

What changed in v4.0 (2026-10-18): every video is a **story with a mystery**, not a question. Biku: the opening questions were "so weird that no one can relate", "these questions are not stories that anyone would watch, there is nothing interesting", and "it should always be stories with mystery and other things that keep people hooked". So the video no longer opens on a question. It opens **inside the story, on its strangest moment**, shown on screen with one line that states it, and the viewer stays to find out how it happened. The topics are stories from everyday life (friends, family, love, neighbours, money between people) with a real result about human behaviour hidden underneath, revealed only at the end. Kept from v3.1: plain words, the characters carry the story, and every explaining line is shown on screen as it is said. Earlier versions are in `prompts/old/`.

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger scrolling past, who has never seen this channel and knows nothing about game theory, must be hooked by the first picture, understand every sentence the first time they hear it, and see on screen what that sentence says at the same moment.**

If a line is clever but a stranger would not follow it, rewrite it plainly. If a word does not move the story or explain it, cut it. If a moment would not make a stranger want to see the next one, it is not strong enough.

## 2. What the video is

A two-minute vertical cartoon that tells one short story about ordinary people, with a mystery at its heart.

- **It opens on the mystery:** the first thing the viewer sees is the strangest moment of the story, something nobody would expect a person to do: a man throws a hundred dollars into a river; an old man has stared at the same phone number for forty years; a woman who watered a garden every evening for two years suddenly stops. The viewer has to know why.
- **It is a thriller:** the pressure rises with every step. Someone has something to lose (money, a friend, love, pride, a home), and there is a clock, a crowd, a price going up, or a door closing.
- **It means something:** the answer to the mystery is a truth about people that the viewer recognises in their own life. The ending should leave them thinking about someone they know.
- **It is true underneath:** the story is made up, but what it shows is a real result of game theory or a real experiment about human behaviour, told in one or two lines at the very end.

**Who is in it:**

- **The Trainer** is the narrator: a calm man's voice. He sets the scene, says what each person wants, and at the end tells what the story really shows. He is dry and a little knowing, never preachy. He speaks in full plain sentences, and he never narrates what the characters can say themselves.
- **The people in the story** are two or three of the channel's regulars. Call them "this man", "this woman", "his best friend" in the story; the viewer does not need their names.
  - **The Saint** (she; glasses, blue scarf): trusts people and plays fair.
  - **The Copycat** (he; orange beanie): does what everyone else does.
  - **The Cheat** (he; red striped tie): takes what he can.
  - **You** (he; green cap): the viewer's own figure. He never speaks.
  - They can play any role the story needs (friends, neighbours, a couple, brothers), as long as each acts in his or her nature. Use at least two of the three (the Saint, the Copycat, the Cheat) in every video.
- **Others** (a child, an old woman, a waiter, a crowd) may appear when the story truly needs them, and may say a short line.
- **The characters talk to each other** in their own voices, in whole sentences, the way people really talk. The story is carried by what they say and do.
- **A voice from outside** may read one piece of advice near the end. It is a different voice from everyone in the story.

## 3. How to write every line

1. **Full sentences.** Every line has someone doing something. Never a fragment such as "Smoke. Silence. Nobody moves."
2. **No random words.** No poetic lines, no mood words that mean nothing ("the darkness hungers"), no insider labels. Every line either moves the story forward or explains it.
3. **Say what is happening and why.** Each step answers "why does this happen now?" If a person does something, the line before or after says what they want.
4. **Introduce before you use.** Every person, thing, rule and number is introduced in plain words before anything depends on it.
5. **Every "he", "she", "they" and "it" has an owner** the viewer has just heard.
6. **Every number says what it is a number of**, and is said the way people say it: "ninety dollars", "one in two", "about half".
7. **One idea to a sentence,** up to about 18 words.
8. **Join the steps with "so", "but" and "because".** Each thing that happens is caused by the one before.
9. **A thought** is written `CHEAT (thinks): If he says no, we both get nothing. He won't say no.` It is heard while the mouth stays shut. Use two or three, at the moments a person decides something.
10. **No questions as hooks.** The story hooks by what happens, not by asking. The only question in a video is the one moment the story stops to ask the viewer what they would do (step 4), and even that is about the choice in the story, never about a study.
11. No em dashes. Mark one important word a line with asterisks for the captions: `He kept *ninety.*`

## 4. Keeping people hooked

1. **The strangest moment first.** Open on the picture nobody expects, with one line that states it plainly. No question, no greeting, no "in this video".
2. **A promise in the second line:** something that makes it stranger or says there is more ("And he did it on purpose.").
3. **A small mystery inside every step.** Each step answers one question and opens the next. The viewer always wants the next ten seconds.
4. **Something to lose, and a clock.** Say early what is at stake, and make it bigger at every step.
5. **A turn in the middle.** Something the viewer did not see coming: a choice, a betrayal, a kindness.
6. **The answer arrives late,** in step 5, and it answers the mystery the video opened on exactly.
7. **The rule of the game is said once, plainly**, before the trap closes: who can do what, and what happens if they say no.
8. **Every choice has its reason on screen:** a thought, a line of dialogue, or the Trainer saying what the person wants.
9. **One small real detail in each scene** anyone would recognise: a phone lighting up, a glass of water, someone checking the time.
10. **Show, then say.** Write what we see in round brackets, then the line. About one action line for every two spoken lines.
11. **Every explaining line is shown, not only told.** When the Trainer explains (the rule, what happened, the real result), the picture shows exactly what he says, at the moment he says it, each thing arriving on the word that names it: `[SHOW: what we see, each piece with (on: word)]`. With the sound off, the pictures alone should tell the story.

## 5. The six steps, in this order

About 260 to 300 spoken words in all. That is two minutes.

| Step                  | About      | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The strange moment | 10 seconds | The story's strangest moment on screen, with one line that states it, at most 12 words ("He threw a hundred dollars into the river."), shown as big words. Then one line that makes it stranger or promises more. |
| 2. Rewind             | 20 seconds | Earlier: who these people are, what each one wants, what is at stake, and the one rule of their situation, said plainly.                                                                                          |
| 3. The trap closes    | 40 seconds | The characters talk and choose. Each choice makes it worse, the pressure rises, and there is a turn the viewer did not see coming.                                                                                |
| 4. The choice         | 10 seconds | The story stops just before the decision. The Trainer asks the viewer what they would do in this person's place, and the picture holds in silence.                                                                |
| 5. The answer         | 25 seconds | What the person did, and why: the answer to the mystery the video opened on. Then, in one or two lines, the real experiment or game behind it: who found it, when, and what they found.                           |
| 6. The meaning        | 15 seconds | Where the viewer does the same thing in their own life, and one last dry line that leaves them thinking.                                                                                                          |

The first line states the strange moment; the last line tells what it meant. Together they should make a stranger want to send the video to someone.

## 6. What the studio can show

You do not write camera directions. Write the words, and the studio chooses the pictures. You may ask for these, in square brackets after a line:

- `[BIG WORDS]` the key words of the line large on screen. The first line always has it.
- `[SHOW: what we see, each piece with (on: word)]` for every line that explains: the story's own people and things moving to show it (a row of people growing, money passing from one person to another, a count before and after, time passing). This channel does not use charts.
- `[NUMBER: $90]` one large number, with a few words under it.
- `[CARD: up to four short lines]` a card on the screen, for advice near the end.
- `[WHAT YOU GET: label / label]` two outcomes shown side by side as pictures. Once, where the result is told.
- `[EVERYONE]` the camera pulls back from the person to a whole crowd doing the same thing. Once, on the line that says "you do this too".
- `[REACTION: greed]` or `[REACTION: shock]` one big cartoon reaction on the moment the story turns. Once a video at most.
- `[PAUSE]` after the Trainer's question in step 4.
- `[TIME PASSES: from ... to ...]` days, seasons or years going by in one place (leaves falling, people growing older).
- `[REWIND: 10 MINUTES EARLIER]` at the start of step 2: the opening plays backwards, fast, like an old film reel running back, and stops on the start of the story with those words stamped on it. Up to 28 letters.

And you may describe an action on its own line in round brackets, in plain words: `(The man walks to the river and opens his hand.)`

People can sit, stand, walk in or out, run, reach across a table, point, shrug, cross their arms, slump, put a hand on their heart, hold something out, and look pleased, worried, suspicious, afraid, shocked, sly, furious, sad or beaten. They can be children, adults or old.

Places already drawn: a street by day or night, a city street, a living room with a sofa, a home, a restaurant, a daycare front, an office, a classroom, a game-show stage, an auction room, a rope bridge over a gorge at night, and a dark room with one bare bulb. If the story needs another place or thing, list it under NEEDS DRAWING: one or two simple things.

Do not write: more than four people we need to see in one scene, fights or injury, anyone shown hurt, real living people as characters, or any real brand.

## 7. The facts

- Build the video on one real study, game or experiment about human behaviour, with a result that surprises. Only situations where people want different things: never a puzzle with one right answer.
- In step 5 say it was real, say the year and who did it, and give what they found in full plain sentences.
- Every number is the source's own. If it is rounded, say "about" or "nearly". If you are not sure of a figure, do not use it.
- The story is made up to show the idea. Never claim it happened exactly this way.
- End with SOURCES: the researchers, the title and the year, and one line on what was found. Give a link only if you are certain it is real.

## 8. Read it as a stranger before you hand it over

1. Would the first picture and line stop me scrolling? Do I need to know what happened?
2. Do I know who is talking, and who or what they are talking about?
3. Is every line a whole sentence, in words I use every day?
4. Does every line move the story or explain it? If not, cut it.
5. After step 2, could I tell a friend what is at stake and the rule of their situation?
6. After step 3, do I know why each person did what they did?
7. Does step 5 answer the mystery from the first line exactly?
8. With the sound off, would the pictures alone tell me what each explaining line says?
9. Would I send this to someone it reminds me of?

If any answer is no, rewrite that part.

## 9. A finished example

This is the standard. The story hooks by what happens; the study only arrives at the end.

```
TITLE: He Threw $100 Into the River on Purpose
THE STRANGE MOMENT IN ONE SENTENCE: A man throws a hundred dollar note into a river, on purpose, while his friend watches.
THE REAL RESULT: In the ultimatum game, people very often turn down an unfair share, even if it means they get nothing at all.
WHO IS IN IT: the Copycat, the Cheat
THE PLACE: a city street by a river, evening
NEEDS DRAWING: a river with a low wall beside the street

1. THE STRANGE MOMENT
(Evening. A man in an orange beanie holds a hundred dollar note over a river. His friend in a red tie stares at him.)
TRAINER: He threw a hundred dollars into the river. [BIG WORDS]
(He opens his hand. The note floats away.)
TRAINER: And he had every reason to keep *some* of it.

2. REWIND
[REWIND: 10 MINUTES EARLIER]
(The two men walk home from work along the river.)
TRAINER: Ten minutes earlier, these two friends were walking home from work.
(The man in the red tie stops and picks up a note from the pavement.)
CHEAT: Look at this. A hundred *dollars.*
COPYCAT: We found it together. Let's split it.
CHEAT: Here's my rule. I say how we split it. If you say no, it goes in the *river.* [SHOW: the note held over the water (on: river)]
TRAINER: So the rule was simple. One man offers a split, the other says yes or no, and no means nobody gets *anything.*

3. THE TRAP CLOSES
CHEAT (thinks): If he says no, he gets nothing. Ten dollars is better than nothing. He'll take it.
CHEAT: I keep ninety. You get *ten.* [NUMBER: $90 / $10]
COPYCAT: Ten? We found it *together.*
CHEAT: It's ten dollars you didn't have this morning. Take it or leave it.
(The man in the beanie looks at the note, then at his friend.)
TRAINER: Ten dollars is free money. Any calculator would say take it.
COPYCAT (thinks): If I take it, he'll do this to me again. Every time.

4. THE CHOICE
TRAINER: Ten dollars, or nothing at all. What would you do? [PAUSE]
(Silence. The river runs.)

5. THE ANSWER
(The man in the beanie takes the note from his friend's hand and holds it over the water.)
COPYCAT: Then we both get *nothing.*
(He opens his hand. The note floats away. The man in the red tie stares after it.) [REACTION: shock]
TRAINER: He did not throw away ten dollars. He threw away ninety of his friend's. [SHOW: the note on the water, its ninety and ten marked (on: ninety)]
TRAINER: This is not just a story. In 1982, three German economists first played this game with real money.
TRAINER: It has been played thousands of times since. When people are offered a fifth or less, about half of them say *no.* [SHOW: a row of ten people, five tearing up their small share (on: half)]

6. THE MEANING
TRAINER: You do this too. A small raise after years of work, an unfair share of the chores: people walk away from a little to make a point. [EVERYONE]
TRAINER: He paid ten dollars to be treated fairly. And his friend never offered him ten again.

SOURCES:
- Werner Güth, Rolf Schmittberger and Bernd Schwarze, "An Experimental Analysis of Ultimatum Bargaining", Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization, 1982. The first experiment of the ultimatum game.
- Colin Camerer, Behavioral Game Theory, 2003. Across many studies, offers of about a fifth or less are rejected about half the time.
```

(The example's facts must still be checked against the sources before a video is made from it.)

## 10. The layout to deliver

Use the same layout as the example: TITLE, THE STRANGE MOMENT IN ONE SENTENCE, THE REAL RESULT, WHO IS IN IT, THE PLACE, NEEDS DRAWING, the six headed steps, each line starting with who speaks (TRAINER, SAINT, COPYCAT, CHEAT, OUTSIDE VOICE, or an extra such as WAITER), actions in round brackets on their own lines, and SOURCES at the end. Then add:

- **YOUTUBE:** a title that states the strange moment, not a question ("He Threw $100 Into the River on Purpose"), two plain lines of description, one line naming the real source, three to five hashtags.
- **FACEBOOK CAPTION:** one or two plain lines that tell the strange moment and stop before the answer.

1. She Wrote Down Every Dish She Washed for a Year

The strange moment: She wrote down every dish she washed, for a whole year.

The story: A couple keeps having the same fight: “I do everything in this house.” Fed up, she starts a secret notebook and logs every dish, every bin, every load of washing. After a year she slams it on the table as proof. He goes quiet, opens a drawer, and pulls out his own notebook. His says he did most of it too.

The turn: Neither of them was lying. Both notebooks are honest.

The meaning: We remember our own work because we were there for it, and miss most of what the other person did.

The real result: Ross and Sicoly, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1979. When spouses rated their own share of chores like tidying and taking out the rubbish, their combined estimates came to more than 100%.
