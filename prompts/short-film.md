# Animated short films: how to write a script

## Script guide, first edition (v1.0)

For Biku: paste everything under the line into whichever AI writes the script, and fill in the INPUTS at the bottom. It carries over what the game theory videos taught the studio (plain storytelling a stranger follows, feelings that change only for a reason, setting up what pays off, nothing the studio cannot draw), but none of that niche's own rules: no Trainer, no dark room, no six fixed steps. It is for the animated short films only.

---

## 1. The one rule

**A stranger who has never seen this channel must understand every moment of the film the first time they watch it, on a phone, with the sound on or off.**

If a moment is beautiful but a stranger would not understand what just happened or why someone feels what they feel, rewrite it so they would.

## 2. What the film is

A short animated film of two to four minutes about one person and one feeling: love, loss, fear, hope, pride, shame, courage. Small, true, everyday stakes told with care, not big plots.

- **Drawn in a simple cartoon style:** round white or skin-coloured heads, thin bodies, clothes, clear faces. Think of a quiet picture book, not an action film.
- **One to three people who matter**, and at most two others in the background. Give each a name for yourself (Grandpa, the boy), but the viewer learns who they are from what they do.
- **One to three places.** Each scene happens in one place, and the film moves through them in order.
- **Usually no dialogue.** A film without speech travels in every language and never sounds like a computer voice. If speech is truly needed, use at most five short lines in the whole film, each a whole plain sentence.
- **Words on screen:** the title at the end, and at most one short closing line. Nothing else.

## 3. Telling it without words

With nobody explaining, everything the viewer needs must be seen or heard:

1. **Every important thing happens on screen, plainly and slowly enough to see.** Not "he realises she is gone" but "he reaches for her cup; the chair beside him is empty; he holds the cup and does not drink."
2. **Each moment is caused by the one before.** Join your scenes with "so" and "but", never "and then". If a scene could be moved without breaking the story, it does not belong.
3. **One action at a time.** Give every action two to four seconds on screen. A scene of thirty seconds holds about eight actions, no more.
4. **Show the feeling, then the reason, then the change.** A face changes only when something visible causes it: a sound, an object, what someone else does. Write the cause in the same line as the change.
5. **One line of feeling for each person in each scene.** Write it down (see the feeling plan in section 7). No face changes back and forth without a reason.
6. **Plant two things early that pay off at the end:** an object (a pencil, a hat, a key) and a habit (a song, a gesture, a way of drawing the sun). The ending uses both, changed.
7. **Give the turn a silence.** Just before the moment everything changes, stop: no music, one sound, a held picture for two or three seconds.
8. **Quiet beats loud.** No one screams, no one sobs out loud, no villain. Small gestures carry more: a hand that stops, a look away, a smile that takes a moment to come.

## 4. The shape

| Part                      | About | What it must do                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------- | ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Who and what they love | 20%   | Show the person, who or what they care about, and the two things planted for later. The first three seconds must already show who the film is about. |
| 2. Something goes wrong   | 20%   | The first small sign of the problem. Nobody understands it yet; the viewer half does.                                                                |
| 3. It gets worse          | 25%   | The problem grows, and the person fights it the only way they can. One moment of hope that it might work.                                            |
| 4. The turn               | 15%   | The silence, then the hardest moment: they lose, or they choose, or they let go.                                                                     |
| 5. After                  | 20%   | What stays: the planted object and habit come back, changed, often with time passed. End on a picture, not on an explanation.                        |

The film answers one question the viewer feels from the start ("will he keep his grandfather?"). The ending answers it, even if the answer is no.

## 5. What the studio can draw well

Write for these. Anything else, ask for in NOTES before building it into the story.

- **People:** standing, walking, running, sitting, kneeling, lying down, hugging, pointing, waving, hands up, arms crossed, slumping, looking up or down. Children, adults, the elderly. Clothes, hats, glasses, scarves.
- **Faces:** happy, sad, afraid, surprised, angry, thinking, worried, hopeful, guilty, lost, tired, crying. They change slowly and for a reason.
- **Things:** most everyday objects a hand can hold or a table can carry; a letter, a pencil, a cup, a photo, a hat, a bag, a phone.
- **Places:** a street by day or night, a field, a forest, a home, a living room, a classroom, a city street, the sea, space; a plain page that the place is drawn onto.
- **Effects:** lines that draw themselves, parts of a picture fading or being rubbed out, rain, smoke, light changing, time passing (a fade).
- **Camera:** wide, medium, close on a face, only the eyes, slow push in, slow pull back, slow drift.

**Avoid:** crowds that act, fast fights or chases, fine finger work (tying a lace, writing words that must be read), animals as main characters, water or fire that people touch, mouths that must say words in close-up, anything that needs real-looking textures.

## 6. Sound, written in words

For each scene write what is heard, simply:

- **Music:** one theme for the whole film, described in plain words (a soft piano tune). Say how it changes: returns, goes slightly wrong, drops to a single note, comes back fuller. Music runs under the whole film except where silence is the point.
- **Sounds:** the few real sounds that matter (a pencil scratching, a door, rain, a cup set down).
- A laugh, a giggle or a sigh can be written, but say so: it may need a real recording.

## 7. Read it as a stranger, and check it, before you hand it over

1. **Stranger test:** after each scene, could someone who knows nothing tell a friend who this is, what they want and what just happened?
2. **Cause test:** for every change of face, is its cause on screen in the same moment?
3. **Count test:** count the actions in each scene; no more than one for every three or four seconds.
4. **Pay-off test:** do both planted things come back at the end, changed?
5. **Studio test:** is everything in section 5? List anything that is not under NOTES TO THE STUDIO.
6. **Plain test:** no em dashes; the closing line, if any, is one plain sentence a child would understand, specific to this story, not a general moral.

## 8. A finished example (how it reads)

> **SCENE 3: The Sun Without a Face (1:05 to 1:30)**
> **Visual:** Back on the bench. Grandpa lifts his finger to draw the sun, like always. He draws the circle and stops. He stares at it. The sun hangs there, blank.
> The boy waits. Then he quietly reaches up with the pencil and adds the smile himself. Grandpa looks at the sun, then at the boy, and pats his head. His eyes still look far away.
> **Feelings:** Grandpa content, then lost when the circle is done and nothing comes. The boy happy, worried a beat after Grandpa stops, then set as he draws.
> **Sound:** the piano drops to a single held note; silence after the smile is drawn.

It works because each action is visible, each feeling has its cause on screen, and the pencil and the smiling sun were set up in scene 1.

## 9. The layout to deliver

1. **TITLE** (one or two words), **LOGLINE** (one sentence: who, what they want, what is in the way), **LENGTH**, **DIALOGUE:** none, or the lines.
2. **PLANTED:** the object and the habit, and where each pays off.
3. **SCENES**, each with: a heading with its time range; **Visual** (what we see, one action per sentence); **Feelings** (each person's line of feeling in this scene, with the cause of any change); **Sound** (music and sounds).
4. **CLOSING WORDS ON SCREEN:** the one line, if any, and the title card.
5. **FEELING PLAN:** for each person, one line per scene: their feeling, and what changes it.
6. **THUMBNAIL:** the studio's short film design. The picture is split on a slant into how the film starts (dark) and how it ends (bright), the same person on both sides, one object from the story on the split, the title across the top in two or three words with one word in gold. Say which object, which two moments and which word is gold.
7. **YOUTUBE:** the title as "TITLE - An Animated Short Film", two plain lines of description, three to five hashtags.
8. **NOTES TO THE STUDIO:** anything outside section 5.

## 10. INPUTS

- **IDEA:** (a feeling, a situation, or a whole story)
- **LENGTH:** (two to four minutes)
- **DIALOGUE:** (none, or a few lines)
- **WHO:** (ages and relationships, if you have them in mind)
- **EARLIER FILMS:** (titles, so nothing repeats)
- **NOTES:** (anything else)
