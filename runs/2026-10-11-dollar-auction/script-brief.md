## How this one is told
The whole video happens in one auction room at night. The storyteller is not neutral: he is the auctioneer, he enjoys every bid, and halfway through the viewer finds out that he is the one walking away with the money. The dollar bill sits under the lamp the whole time while the tag on it climbs past its own value. Near the end he admits, unwillingly, that someone worked out how to beat him.

**Caption emphasis:** $1.85 for $1 / For nothing

### Beat 2. The tiny rule (0:10 to 0:27). Spine step: Why it matters
**NARRATOR:** Rewind. One dollar. Bids start at a nickel. Highest bid takes it.
**NARRATOR:** One tiny rule. Second place pays too, and gets nothing.
**NARRATOR:** A dollar for a nickel. Would you raise your hand?
**THE CHEAT:** Five cents.
**THE COPYCAT:** Ten cents.
**On screen:** The scene zips backward. The bill is back on the podium under the lamp, tag blank. A card on the podium reads: SECOND PLACE PAYS TOO.
Cut to You alone, green cap, hand hovering half raised. Hold for a beat of silence. Then the Cheat lifts a paddle marked 5¢ and the Copycat copies the same move with 10¢.
**Sound:** Tape rewind whirr, then two soft paddle taps.
**Caption emphasis:** Second place pays too / Would you raise your hand?

### Beat 3. Past a dollar (0:27 to 0:48). Spine step: First half
**NARRATOR:** Ninety-five cents from the Cheat. One dollar from the Copycat.
**THE CHEAT:** If I stop, I lose ninety-five cents. If I bid a dollar five and win, I only lose a nickel.
**THE COPYCAT:** If I stop, I lose a dollar. A dollar ten.
**NARRATOR:** Nobody is chasing the dollar now. They just can't afford to come second.
**On screen:** A scoreboard over the podium: CHEAT 95¢, COPYCAT $1.00. The Cheat freezes, counts on his fingers, then slams the paddle: $1.05. The room turns to the danger palette as the board passes $1.00.
The Copycat repeats the Cheat's exact finger count and raises: $1.10. On "come second" both are sweating and staring at each other. The bill sits ignored between them.
**Sound:** A ticking that speeds up with each bid.
**Caption emphasis:** $1.05 for $1 / Can't afford to come second

## Why it passes the five tests
- **TRUE:** Every real figure has a source below. The opening sale is an invented scene, and its two bids add up to an amount inside the range the game's own author reported. Uncertainty is carried in the lines themselves: "said to", "he wrote", "one study counted".
- **CLEAR:** In one plain sentence: would you bid a nickel for a dollar bill if second place also has to pay? No metaphor is needed, and the story never leaves the auction room except for three short visits.
- **NEW:** The search turned up many written explainers, and they tell it the same way: the walk from a nickel to a dollar five, ending on "the only way to win is not to play". This script is told by the seller instead, adds the real website's numbers, and ends on the sixty cent solution, which says the well-known ending is not quite right. The search returned articles, not a list of videos, so how many videos already use this angle is not known.
- **SURPRISING:** Five turns: the author said to play it at parties; twenty dollars sold for two thousand; the storyteller is the winner; a real site made twenty-six million and sold cash for double; and perfect logic ends the game after one sixty cent bid.
- **IMMERSIVE:** The viewer is asked to raise a hand, watches two players reason themselves past a dollar, and then learns the voice in their ear has been collecting the money. Nothing is defined or listed.

## Notes on accuracy
- **Invented:** The opening sale ($1.85 and $1.80) and every bid the cast makes. They are a made-up scene. The two final bids were chosen so they total $3.65, inside the three to five dollar range Shubik reported.
- **"Published", not "invented":** The script says the game was published in 1971. It may be older than the paper, so the line avoids saying when it was invented.
- **"Said to play it at a party":** Shubik's words are that experience showed the best time is during a party when spirits are high. The line shortens that.
- **"Not unusual":** Shubik's words are "not uncommon", and he means the two payments added together.
- **Calculated by me:** "A hundred times its worth" is $2,000 divided by $20. "About two dollars for every dollar" rounds the study's 204%. The 60 cent figure was checked against O'Neill's formula as quoted in later papers, and it comes out the same.
- **The website is a cousin, not a twin.** It was a penny auction site called Swoopo, not named in the spoken lines. There, every bid cost a fee (75 cents in the US), where in the dollar auction only the top two bidders pay. The study treats the two as close relatives, which is why the line says "a close cousin".
- **The $26 million** is the study's own cautious estimate for the four years of data. The same study found the site lost money on slightly more than half of its single auctions and made it back on the rest. The company went bankrupt in 2011. None of that is in the spoken lines.
- **"Perfect logic"** is shorthand. O'Neill's answer holds under his conditions: two players, the same known budget each, and players who never choose at random. Some later writers argue it does not carry over to a game with no spending limit.
- **"The trap was the second bid"** is this script's one-line reading of O'Neill's result together with Shubik's remark that escalation becomes real once two bids are in.
- **Left out as unconfirmed:** A well-known story that a professor made $17,000 auctioning $20 bills to students, with one bill going for $204. It turned up only in second-hand retellings, so it is not in the script.
- **Common figures:** none used.
- **The group chat ending** is a comparison, and the cost there is time, not money.
- **Research limit:** The run stopped at the fifteen lookups this prompt allows. Two sources were read in full and the rest were read from search result extracts, as marked in the table.
