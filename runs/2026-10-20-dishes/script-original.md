TITLE: She Wrote Down Every Dish She Washed for a Year
THE STRANGE MOMENT IN ONE SENTENCE: A woman has written down every dish she washed for a whole year, and she is saving the notebook for one night.
THE REAL RESULT: When husbands and wives each judge their own share of the housework, their two shares add up to more than the whole job, because each person remembers their own work best.
WHO IS IN IT: the Saint (the wife), the Copycat (the husband)
THE PLACE: their kitchen at home, at night
NEEDS DRAWING: a kitchen with a sink and a stack of dishes; two small notebooks, one blue and one orange

1. THE STRANGE MOMENT
(Midnight. A woman in glasses and a blue scarf stands at a kitchen sink. She puts down a clean plate, picks up a small blue notebook and writes in it.)
TRAINER: She wrote down every dish she washed, for a whole year. [BIG WORDS]
[SHOW: the notebook page filling with tally marks, the number 4,212 at the bottom (on: every dish)]
TRAINER: And she was saving every one of them for *one night.*

2. REWIND
[REWIND: ONE YEAR EARLIER]
(The same kitchen. The woman and a man in an orange beanie stand on either side of a full sink.)
TRAINER: One year earlier, this married couple had the same fight every week.
SAINT: I do *everything* in this house.
COPYCAT: Everything? I do plenty. You just don't see it.
SAINT: Then show me.
COPYCAT: Show you what? Nobody keeps a *list.*
(He walks out. She stares at the sink.)
TRAINER: It was always her word against *his.* [SHOW: two speech bubbles pushing against each other, neither one moving (on: her word)]
TRAINER: So she made her own rule. Whoever has it in writing *wins.* [SHOW: a small blue notebook landing on the counter (on: notebook)]
TRAINER: On their anniversary, she would put the proof on the table.

3. THE TRAP CLOSES
SAINT (thinks): He can argue with me. He can't argue with a year of *proof.*
[TIME PASSES: from winter to autumn in the kitchen]
(She writes after every dish, every trash bag, every towel she folds.)
TRAINER: Every night the number got *bigger.* [SHOW: the tally marks spreading across page after page (on: bigger)]
(One night she comes down for a glass of water. The man is at the kitchen table, writing in something small. He sees her and slides it into a drawer.)
COPYCAT: It's nothing. Go back to bed.
SAINT (thinks): Nothing? He's hiding *something.*
(Anniversary night. Two plates of dinner on the table. She drops the blue notebook between them.)
SAINT: Four thousand two hundred and twelve dishes. Every one of them was *me.* [NUMBER: 4,212 / dishes, all hers]
(The man looks down at the notebook. He says nothing.)

4. THE CHOICE
TRAINER: A year of proof, in her own handwriting. So who really did more? [PAUSE]
(Silence. The kitchen clock ticks.)

5. THE ANSWER
(The man stands, opens the drawer, and puts a small orange notebook next to hers.)
COPYCAT: I saw you writing, so I started one too. The same *week.*
(She opens it. Page after page of his handwriting.) [REACTION: shock]
TRAINER: His notebook said that *he* did most of the work. [SHOW: his pages, "fixed the boiler, 2 a.m." and "walked the dog in the rain" (on: most)]
SAINT: You fixed the boiler at two in the morning? I never *knew.*
COPYCAT: You washed my work shirts by hand? I never *saw.*
TRAINER: Neither of them was lying. Each one only saw their *own* half. [SHOW: the kitchen split down the middle, each person lit only on their own side (on: own)]
TRAINER: This is not just a story. In 1979, two Canadian psychologists asked thirty seven married couples who did each job at home.
TRAINER: The jobs went from making breakfast to starting the *arguments.*
TRAINER: Each spouse answered alone. Added together, their two shares usually came to more than the whole *job.* [SHOW: one plate cut into his share and her share, the two shares too big to fit back on the plate (on: more)]
TRAINER: In early tests, couples compared their answers at home and *fought.* So the researchers changed the questions.

6. THE MEANING
TRAINER: You do this too, at work and with roommates. You remember what you did, because you were *there.* [EVERYONE]
TRAINER: Both notebooks were honest. It just took two of them to see the whole *house.*

SOURCES:
- Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly, "Egocentric Biases in Availability and Attribution", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, volume 37, issue 3, pages 322 to 336, 1979. Five experiments, including 37 married couples who separately rated each spouse's share of everyday jobs (from making breakfast to causing arguments); each person recalled more of their own contributions and claimed more of the credit than their partner gave them, so the two self-estimates typically added up to more than the whole. The paper also reports that in pilot tests with percentages, couples remembered and compared their answers afterwards and it caused conflict, so the final study had them mark a line instead. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.3.322
- Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011. Retells the study: the spouses' self-assessed shares added up to more than 100 percent.
