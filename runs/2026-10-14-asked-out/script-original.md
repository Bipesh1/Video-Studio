TITLE: How to Make Sure You Never Get Asked Out
THE TRAP IN ONE SENTENCE: When you wait to be chosen, you only get to pick from the people who chose you.
WHO IT HAPPENS TO: the Saint. The Cheat is the one who walks up and asks.
BACK ROW: four guests standing along the wall at the party. They do not work for the Trainer. One of them is the man the Saint likes.
IN THE ROOM: a cup, a phone.
NEEDS DRAWING: nothing

1. THE QUESTION
TRAINER: Would you rather ask someone out, or wait to be *asked?* [BIG WORDS]
TRAINER: Most people wait. Watch what it costs.

2. THE SET-UP
TRAINER: This is a friend's party.
(The woman stands near the wall, holding a cup.)
TRAINER: This woman came hoping one man would come and talk to her.
TRAINER: He is standing at the end of the wall. She has liked him for *months.*
SAINT (thinks): If he likes me, he will come over. I just have to *wait.*
TRAINER: What she does not know is that he is waiting for *her.*

3. IT STARTS
(The man in the red tie walks straight up to her.)
TRAINER: Now this man walks up. She is the one he wanted most, so he *asks.*
CHEAT: Hi. I've wanted to talk to you all night. Can I get you a drink?
SAINT (thinks): He is not the one I wanted. But at least *somebody* asked.
SAINT: Sure. Thank you.
TRAINER: See what she did? She did not choose from everyone here. She chose from whoever *came to her.*

4. IT GETS WORSE
(She looks over at the man by the wall. He stays where he is.)
CHEAT: Want to go sit by the window and talk?
SAINT: I was sort of waiting for someone.
CHEAT: Well, they're not over here. *I am.*
TRAINER: While she waits, the only choices she gets are the people brave enough to *ask.*
TRAINER: What do you think she *does?*
SAINT (thinks): He is never coming. This one is here. That is *enough.*
(She follows him to the window.)
TRAINER: She *settles.* And the man she wanted never moves at all.

5. IT WAS REAL
(Back at the table, under the bare bulb.)
TRAINER: This is not a story. In 1962, two mathematicians, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, *proved it.* [NUMBER: 1962]
TRAINER: When one side asks and the other side waits, the people who ask get the best partner they could realistically get.
TRAINER: The people who wait get the *worst.*
TRAINER: In 2012, this work won a *Nobel Prize.* [NUMBER: 2012]
TRAINER: Today it matches young doctors to American hospitals. And the doctors do the *asking.*
TRAINER: You do this too. You wait for the text, the invite, the raise, and you take what comes.

6. THE WAY OUT
OUTSIDE VOICE: Here is how you beat it. If you want someone, do not wait to be noticed. [CARD: Want someone? / Do not wait to be noticed. / Ask first. / A no is not the end.]
OUTSIDE VOICE: Ask first. You will hear some no's, but every yes comes from someone *you chose.*
TRAINER: So should you wait to be asked? Only if you want whoever is *left.*

SOURCES:
- David Gale and Lloyd S. Shapley, "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage", The American Mathematical Monthly, 1962. Showed that when one side does the asking and the other side holds its best offer, the asking side ends up with the best partner it could have in any stable match.
- D. G. McVitie and L. B. Wilson, "The Stable Marriage Problem", Communications of the ACM, 1971. Showed the other side of the same result: the side that waits ends up with the worst partner it could have in any stable match.
- The 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences went to Alvin E. Roth and Lloyd S. Shapley for the theory of stable matching and its use in designing real markets.
- Alvin E. Roth and Elliott Peranson, "The Redesign of the Matching Market for American Physicians", American Economic Review, 1999. The US medical residency match switched to a version where the doctors do the asking, first used in March 1998.
