TITLE: How to Make Sure You Never Get Asked Out
THE TRAP IN ONE SENTENCE: People who wait to be chosen only get to pick from whoever comes to them, so they end up with less than the people who ask.
WHO IT HAPPENS TO: the Saint. The Copycat is the man she likes.
BACK ROW: four other guests at the party. They do not work for the Trainer.
IN THE ROOM: a cup, a phone.
THE REAL PLACE: a house party on a Saturday night.
NEEDS DRAWING: nothing

1. THE QUESTION
TRAINER: If you liked someone, would you wait for *them* to ask? [BIG WORDS]
TRAINER: You think waiting is safer. Watch this.

2. THE SET-UP
(Music and talk from the rest of the party.)
TRAINER: This is a house party on a Saturday night.
(The woman stands near the front, holding a cup.)
TRAINER: This woman came tonight for one man, the one in the *orange hat.*
(She looks across the room at the man in the orange hat. He catches her eye and smiles.)
SAINT (thinks): If he likes me, he'll come *over.*
(Her phone lights up in her bag. She does not even look at it.)
TRAINER: What she does not know is this. The one who asks gets to *choose.*
TRAINER: The one who waits only gets *chosen.*

3. IT STARTS
TRAINER: So I give her a choice. Walk over, or stay where it feels *safe.*
(She takes one step toward him. Then she stops.)
SAINT (thinks): What if he says no in front of everyone? Better to *wait.*
(She steps back and sips from her cup.)
TRAINER: See what she did? She chose to *wait* to be chosen.

4. IT GETS WORSE
(A clock ticks under the music. People start picking up their coats.)
TRAINER: Now the party is ending, and everyone is *pairing up.*
(The man in the orange hat looks around the room. Then he starts walking her way.)
SAINT: He's coming over. See? Waiting *works.*
TRAINER: Now she is sure waiting works, so she will not *move.*
TRAINER: What do you think he *does?*
(Silence.)
(A woman by the back wall waves at him. He walks straight past the Saint and goes to her.) [REACTION: shock]
TRAINER: Another woman made the *first move,* so he goes to her.
TRAINER: Asking lets you choose from the whole room. Waiting leaves you whoever comes to *you.*
(The music stops. The Saint puts down her cup and checks her phone. There are no new messages.)

5. IT WAS REAL
TRAINER: This is not a story. In 1962, two mathematicians, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, worked out how people pair up. [NUMBER: 1962]
TRAINER: They proved the side that asks gets the best partner it could *keep.*
TRAINER: The side that waits gets the *worst* partner who would still stay. [WHAT YOU GET: ASKED: BEST PARTNER YOU COULD KEEP / WAITED: WORST PARTNER WHO STILL STAYS]
TRAINER: In 2012, that work won a *Nobel Prize.* [NUMBER: 2012]
TRAINER: This year, that same method placed more than thirty-eight thousand young doctors in US hospitals. [NUMBER: 38,000 doctors]
TRAINER: There, the *doctors* do the asking.
TRAINER: You do this too, waiting for the text, the call, the *first move.* [EVERYONE]

6. THE WAY OUT
OUTSIDE VOICE: Here is how you beat it. If you like someone, ask *first.* [CARD: Like someone? / Ask first. / Ask once, kindly. / Take no for an answer.]
OUTSIDE VOICE: Ask once, kindly, and accept a no. A no costs you one *minute.*
TRAINER: So would you wait for them to ask? Only if you want *what is left.*

SOURCES:
- David Gale and Lloyd S. Shapley, "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage", The American Mathematical Monthly, vol. 69, 1962. Showed that when one side does the asking, everyone ends up in pairs nobody wants to break, and every member of the asking side gets the best partner they could have in any such pairing. That the waiting side gets its worst such partner was shown soon after (D. G. McVitie and L. B. Wilson, "The Stable Marriage Problem", Communications of the ACM, 1971).
- The Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel 2012, awarded to Alvin E. Roth and Lloyd S. Shapley for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2012/summary/
- Alvin E. Roth and Elliott Peranson, "The Effects of the Change in the NRMP Matching Algorithm", JAMA, 1997. The US residency match switched to an applicant-proposing version, where the doctors do the asking, starting with the 1998 match.
- National Resident Matching Program, "2026 Main Residency Match By the Numbers". 38,354 applicants matched to first-year positions. https://www.nrmp.org/match-data/2026/03/match-by-the-numbers-2026-main-residency-match/
