# Your First Memory Might Never Have Happened

## The package

TITLE: Your First Memory Might Never Have Happened
THE QUESTION IN A STRANGER'S HEAD: How can I remember something that never happened?
THE PROMISE: You will know why so many people's earliest memories are built later from photos and family stories, and how to test your own.
THE ANSWER IN ONE SENTENCE: A first memory from age two or younger is most likely a real feeling stitched to photos and family stories you met later, and it feels completely real.
THUMBNAIL: a baby in an old pram under a yellow blanket, close and soft-lit, with the words YOUR BRAIN MADE IT UP
THE FIRST TEN SECONDS: the mascot closes his eyes and a fuzzy memory bubble opens above him: himself as a baby in a pram. Narrator: "Think of your very first memory. Got it? There's a good chance it never happened."
WHY A STRANGER CLICKS: everyone has a first memory, and nobody has ever checked whether theirs is real.
KIND: everyday (about you)

THE WORLD: one memory bubble above the mascot's head. The camera goes into the bubble, into his head and his toddler brain, out to a crowd of thousands of people with their own bubbles, round the back of his pram memory, and out to the family photo album and dinner table that supplied the pieces, then back to the bubble.
NEEDS DRAWING: the fuzzy memory bubble; the baby in an old pram under a yellow blanket, holding a cupcake with one candle; a newborn bubble (the mascot in a doctor's hands); the mascot's head opening to a small toddler brain with memory boxes drifting in and fading; an age ruler of three birthday candles marked 1, 2, 3; a crowd of thousands of tiny people, each with a memory bubble; the pram memory seen from high above; the back of a printed family photo with a tiny "Photo: Mom" credit; a family photo album; a dinner table with relatives telling a story; the bubble rebuilt as a collage of cut-out photos.
THE GAUGE: THE CLUES, steps: 1/3 on "Clue one", 2/3 on "Clue two", 3/3 on "Clue three"

## The script

1. THE HOOK

NARRATOR: Think of your very first memory. Got it?
[FLY: close on the mascot, eyes shut | show: a fuzzy memory bubble opening above his head (on: first), inside it a baby in an old pram under a yellow blanket (on: memory)]
(the mascot beams at his baby self, who blows out the one candle on a cupcake)

NARRATOR: There's a good chance it never *happened*.
[FLY: in close to the bubble | title: DID IT REALLY / HAPPEN? | show: the picture flickers and goes grainy like a bad signal (on: never)]

2. THE MOMENT

NARRATOR: Some people even say they remember being *born*.
[FLY: across to a second bubble | show: the mascot as a newborn in a doctor's hands, waving (on: born)]
(the mascot points at it, very proud)

NARRATOR: No, you don't.
[FLY: the newborn bubble pops, and the camera drifts back to the pram bubble]
[BEAT]

3. THE LOOPS

NARRATOR: Clue one: most people's oldest real memory is from around age *three*.
[OPEN: the mascot's head | inside: a small toddler brain (on: memory) | show: an age ruler of three birthday candles, the third one lighting up (on: three)]

NARRATOR: Before that, the brain keeps almost nothing, a blank scientists call *childhood amnesia*.
[FLY: in among the toddler brain | show: memory boxes drift in and fade away before they land (on: nothing) | title: CHILDHOOD / AMNESIA (on: amnesia)]

NARRATOR: Clue two: a British survey asked over six thousand people for their first *memory*.
[FLY: out of the head and up, to a crowd of thousands of tiny people, each with a memory bubble | show: the crowd (on: survey), a callout 6,641 PEOPLE ASKED (on: six)]

NARRATOR: Nearly four in ten said it came from age two or *younger*.
[CALLOUT: 4 IN 10 | caption: SAID AGE 2 OR YOUNGER | at: the crowd, where nearly four in ten bubbles turn yellow | on: four]

NARRATOR: And almost nine hundred said one or younger.
[CALLOUT: 893 | caption: SAID AGE 1 OR YOUNGER | at: a smaller group inside the yellow, glowing brighter | on: nine]
(the mascot, somewhere in the crowd, puts his hand up)
[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Clue three: a lot of those baby memories are about a *pram*.
[FLY: down out of the crowd, back into the mascot's bubble | show: pram bubbles pop up all over the crowd (on: lot), the yellow blanket (on: pram)]

NARRATOR: But look: this one is seen from *above*.
[FLY: round the edge of the bubble, the view tipping until we look straight down into the pram from high overhead (on: above)]

NARRATOR: A baby in a pram could never see that.
[FLY: down into the pram to the baby's eye level, where all you can see is the blanket's edge and the sky (on: never)]
(the mascot squints at his memory, then back at the camera)
[BEAT]

4. THE CATCH

NARRATOR: So here's the catch: the feeling may be real, but the *picture* came later.
[OPEN: the memory bubble, flipped over by the mascot | inside: its back is a printed family photo with a tiny "Photo: Mom" credit in the corner (on: picture)]
(the mascot's face drops)
[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Researchers think it was stitched together from family *photos* and stories told at dinner.
[FLY: out of the photo into a family photo album, then along to a dinner table where relatives are telling a story | show: album (on: photos), dinner table (on: dinner)]

5. THE PROOF

NARRATOR: And it feels just as *real*.
[FLY: back to the bubble, now a collage of cut-out photos stitched together, glowing as warm as before (on: real)]

6. THE PAYOFF

NARRATOR: So is your first memory real? If it's from before age three, probably *not*.
[FLY: back out to the mascot with the collage bubble above him | show: the age ruler of three candles, the bubble sinking below the third one (on: three)]

NARRATOR: So which memories are really *yours*?
[FLY: wide, over thousands of people each with a memory bubble, settling back on the mascot as his eyes close again (on: yours)]

## SOURCES

- Akhtar, Justice, Morrison and Conway, "Fictional First Memories", Psychological Science, 29(10), 2018. Survey of 6,641 people in the UK: average age of first memory 3.2 years; 2,487 (nearly 40%, 38.6%) dated it to age two or younger, 893 to age one or younger; the authors judge these improbable, likely fictional. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618778831
- City, University of London research news on the same study, 2018. The authors suggest these memories are fragments of early experience combined with knowledge from photographs or family conversations; pram memories were a typical example; the person does not know the memory is fictional; such memories were more common in middle-aged and older adults.
