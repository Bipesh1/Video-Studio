TITLE: Why Is the Sun Already Gone When You Watch It Set?
THE QUESTION IN ONE SENTENCE: When the Sun looks like it is sitting on the sea at sunset, where is it really?
THE WORLD: One beach at sunset. The camera starts inside a phone screen filming the Sun, pulls back to the person and the drink beside them, dives into the drink's straw, rises into the sky and cuts the air open into its layers, then turns sideways over the curve of the Earth to ride one beam of sunlight down into the phone and find the real Sun hiding below the horizon, and ends back on the phone screen.

- A phone screen filming a beach sunset, the bottom of the Sun touching the sea
- A faceless figure standing on the sand, holding the phone up
- A glass of water on a towel beside the figure, its straw looking snapped at the waterline
- The sky above the beach cut open to show layers of air: thin and spread out high up, packed tight near the ground
- A side view of the curve of the Earth, with its thin band of air, the beach on top and a low Sun far out (the band of air and the bend of the beam drawn bigger than life so they can be seen)
- One glowing beam of sunlight that curves gently as it comes down through the air
- A glowing picture of the Sun sitting on the horizon, and the real Sun just underneath the horizon line
- The low Sun close up, its round shape pressed into a slight oval, flatter at the bottom

1. THE QUESTION
NARRATOR: Where is the Sun really when it touches the sea at sunset?
[FLY: close on a phone screen filming a beach sunset | show: the Sun (on: Sun), its bottom edge touching the sea (on: sea) | title: WHERE IS THE / SUN REALLY?]
NARRATOR: The answer is in the air around you, and it tricks your *eyes*.
[FLY: back out through the screen to the open beach, the warm air over the sea gently shimmering | show: shimmering air (on: air)]

2. THE MOMENT
NARRATOR: You hold up your phone as the bottom of the Sun touches the sea.
[FLY: further back to a faceless figure standing on the sand | show: phone held up (on: phone), the Sun sitting on the sea (on: bottom)]
NARRATOR: Everyone filming thinks the Sun is right there, sitting on the water.
[CALLOUT: HERE? | caption: WHERE EVERYONE THINKS IT IS | at: the Sun on the sea | on: there]
NARRATOR: And they think its light flies to them in a *straight* line.
[FLY: closer to the sea | show: a dotted straight line drawing itself from the Sun across the water to the phone (on: straight)]
NARRATOR: Both of those ideas are *wrong*.
[FLY: the dotted line and the HERE? label crack and fade away]

3. FOLLOW THE CLUE
NARRATOR: The straw in the drink beside you shows why.
[FLY: down to a glass of water standing on a towel beside the figure | show: straw (on: straw)]
NARRATOR: The straw looks snapped because light changes direction when it crosses from water into air.
[CALLOUT: BENT HERE | caption: LIGHT CHANGES DIRECTION | at: the waterline on the straw | on: direction]
NARRATOR: Air bends light too, because air gets *thicker* the closer it is to the ground.
[OPEN: the sky above the beach | inside: layers of air, thin and spread out at the top, packed tight near the ground (on: thicker)]
NARRATOR: So sunlight curves a little as it passes down through each layer.
[FLY: closer to the opened layers | show: a beam of sunlight coming down through them, turning slightly at each one (on: sunlight)]
NARRATOR: A high Sun's light comes almost straight down, so it barely bends.
[FLY: up to a high Sun overhead | show: its beam dropping straight through the layers (on: high)]
NARRATOR: But a low Sun's light skims sideways through the thickest air, so it bends the *most*.
[FLY: up and turn sideways until the curve of the Earth, its thin band of air and a low Sun are all in view | show: low Sun (on: low), its beam skimming through the band of air (on: skims)]

4. THE CATCH
NARRATOR: Follow one beam from that low Sun as it curves down into your phone.
[FLY: ride along one beam of sunlight as it bends down through the air to the beach | show: beam (on: beam), the phone at the end of it (on: phone)]
NARRATOR: Your eyes and your camera assume that light came in a *straight* line.
[FLY: closer to the phone | show: a straight dotted line running back from the phone along the last part of the beam, up to a glowing Sun sitting on the horizon (on: straight)]
NARRATOR: But trace the curved beam back, and the real Sun is already *below* the horizon.
[FLY: back along the curved beam, past the horizon line | show: the real Sun sitting just underneath it (on: below)]
NARRATOR: The air lifts the picture of the Sun by about its own *width*.
[CALLOUT: 1 SUN | caption: LIFTED BY ITS OWN WIDTH | at: the gap between the real Sun and its glowing picture | on: width]
NARRATOR: So the Sun on your screen has, in a way, already *set*.
[FLY: pull back toward the beach until the glowing Sun on the sea and the real Sun below it are both in view | show: the phone screen in front (on: screen)]

5. THE PROOF
NARRATOR: The British Astronomical Association puts that lift at about half a degree, roughly one whole Sun.
[CALLOUT: 0.5° | caption: ABOUT ONE WHOLE SUN | at: the gap between the two Suns | on: half]
NARRATOR: The Hong Kong Observatory says this makes sunset there about two minutes *later* than with no air.
[CALLOUT: +2 MIN | caption: LATER SUNSET IN HONG KONG | at: the Sun on the sea | on: two]
NARRATOR: And official sunset times already include those extra minutes.
[CALLOUT: SUNSET | caption: INCLUDES THE 2 MINUTES | at: the phone screen | on: official]

6. NOW YOU'LL NOTICE
NARRATOR: You'll also notice a low Sun looks squashed, flat at the bottom.
[FLY: back to the beach and close on the low Sun, its round shape pressed into a slight oval | show: flat bottom edge (on: flat)]
NARRATOR: That is the air lifting its bottom edge more than its top.
[CALLOUT: BOTTOM | caption: LIFTED MORE THAN THE TOP | at: the Sun's bottom edge | on: bottom]
NARRATOR: So when the Sun touches the sea, the real Sun is already just *below* it.
[FLY: back into the phone screen, the Sun sitting on the sea | show: a faint outline of the real Sun just under the waterline (on: below)]

SOURCES:
- British Astronomical Association, "Atmospheric Refraction", 2019. Near the horizon the air lifts the Sun's image by about half a degree, roughly the Sun's width, so a Sun that seems to sit on the horizon is really below it; the bottom edge is lifted more than the top, which makes the Sun look oval. https://britastro.org/2019/atmospheric-refraction
- Hong Kong Observatory (Tong Yu-fai), "Effect of atmospheric refraction on the times of sunrise and sunset", August 2017. The air makes sunset in Hong Kong appear about two minutes later than it would with no air, the real Sun sinks below the horizon before sunset happens, and the Observatory's published sunrise and sunset times already include this. https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/education/astronomy-and-time/astronomy/00493-effect-of-atmospheric-refraction-on-the-times-of-sunrise-and-sunset.html
