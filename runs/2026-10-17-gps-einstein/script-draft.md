TITLE: Why Your Phone's Map Needs Einstein
THE QUESTION IN ONE SENTENCE: How does the blue dot on your map know exactly where you are?
THE WORLD: one journey, from the map on your phone up through the sky to the satellites twenty thousand kilometres up, into one of them, and back down to your hand.
NEEDS DRAWING: drawn for this video already: your phone and its map, the street, the clouds, the whole Earth, the GPS satellites on their orbits, a satellite opening on its atomic clock, the time signals, the four distances, the grid of gravity, two clocks.

1. THE QUESTION
NARRATOR: How does the blue dot on your map know exactly where you are?
[FLY: close on a phone held up in a street, its map with a blue dot | title: HOW DOES THE / BLUE DOT KNOW?]
NARRATOR: The answer is twenty thousand kilometres above your head, where time runs *faster*.
[FLY: up from the phone, past the buildings, through the sky, to the whole Earth | show: satellites (on: twenty)]

2. THE MOMENT
NARRATOR: You might think a satellite up there is watching your phone.
[FLY: closer, to the sky above you | show: a beam from a satellite down to you (on: watching)]
NARRATOR: But your phone never sends it anything. It only *listens*.
[the beam crossed out (on: never), time signals rippling down from the satellites to you (on: listens)]

3. FOLLOW THE CLUE
NARRATOR: About thirty of these satellites circle the Earth, and each one carries a very precise *clock*.
[FLY: out to the whole Earth | CALLOUT: 30 | caption: SATELLITES | at: earth | on: thirty]
NARRATOR: Inside each satellite, an atomic clock counts time by the shaking of atoms.
[FLY: into one satellite | OPEN: its front lifts off | inside: the atomic clock (on: atomic)]
NARRATOR: The satellite keeps sending out its time, and it reaches you about seven hundredths of a second *later*.
[FLY: back out to the sky above you | show: time signals rippling down to you (on: sending) | CALLOUT: 0.07 s | caption: FOR THE SIGNAL / TO REACH YOU | at: you | on: seven]
NARRATOR: Your phone sees how late each time arrives, so it knows how far away each satellite is.
[the signals keep coming down to you]
NARRATOR: With four satellites, only one spot on Earth fits all four distances. That spot is *you*.
[show: the four distances, circles meeting at you (on: four) | CALLOUT: 4 | caption: SATELLITES / ONE SPOT: YOU | at: you | on: you]

4. THE CATCH
NARRATOR: But the clocks up there do not keep the same time as clocks down here.
[FLY: out to the Earth | show: a clock on the ground and a clock in orbit (on: clocks)]
NARRATOR: Gravity slows time down a little, and the satellites are far from the Earth, where gravity is weaker.
[show: the grid of gravity, pulled down round the Earth (on: gravity)]
NARRATOR: Their speed slows them a little too, but each day they still gain about thirty eight millionths of a second.
[CALLOUT: 38 | caption: MILLIONTHS OF A SECOND / GAINED EVERY DAY | at: satellite | on: thirty]
NARRATOR: That sounds like nothing, but a radio signal travels more than eleven kilometres in that time.
[CALLOUT: 11 km | caption: TRAVELLED IN THAT TIME | at: none | on: eleven]
NARRATOR: So if nobody fixed it, your blue dot would be kilometres off within a single *day*.
[FLY: all the way back down to your phone | show: the blue dot sliding away from where you really are (on: kilometres)]

5. THE PROOF
NARRATOR: Albert Einstein's theory of gravity, from 1915, says that time runs faster where gravity is weaker.
[FLY: up again to the Earth in its grid of gravity | CALLOUT: 1915 | caption: EINSTEIN'S THEORY | at: earth | on: 1915]
NARRATOR: So engineers set every satellite clock to tick a little *slow* before it goes up.
[FLY: into the satellite, still open | show: its clock being turned back (on: slow)]
NARRATOR: The physicist Neil Ashby, who worked on GPS, wrote in 2002 that without this, the error would pass eleven kilometres in one day.
[FLY: out to the sky above you | CALLOUT: 11 km | caption: OF ERROR IN ONE DAY / ASHBY, 2002 | at: you | on: eleven]

6. NOW YOU'LL NOTICE
NARRATOR: So every time the blue dot finds you, it is using Einstein's strange idea.
[FLY: down from the satellites, through the sky, to the street | show: the time signals arriving at you (on: every)]
NARRATOR: Your map knows where you are because the clocks in the sky are set for faster *time*.
[FLY: back into the phone in your hand, the blue dot steady]

SOURCES:
- Neil Ashby, "Relativity and the Global Positioning System", Physics Today, vol. 55, no. 5, May 2002, pages 41 to 47. GPS satellites orbit about 20,000 km up with periods of 11 hours 58 minutes, at about 4 km/s; at least four are above the horizon anywhere; a receiver finds its place by solving four equations from the times sent by four satellites; the satellites' clocks run faster than clocks on the ground by about 5 parts in 10 billion from the weaker gravity and slower by a smaller amount from their speed, a net 4.4645 parts in 10 billion (about 38 millionths of a second a day); "satellite clock errors building up in just one day would cause navigational errors of more than 11 km". https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/relativity-and-the-global-positioning-system
- GPS.gov (US government), "Space Segment": satellites at about 20,200 km, each circling the Earth twice a day; 31 operational satellites (July 2023), at least 24 needed. https://www.gps.gov/systems/gps/space/
- Neil Ashby, "Relativity in the Global Positioning System", Living Reviews in Relativity, 2003: the effects must be accounted for or the system would not work. https://link.springer.com/article/10.12942/lrr-2003-1
- Checked by Claude on 2026-10-17. Light travels about 300,000 km a second, so 38 millionths of a second is about 11.4 km; a signal from 20,200 km up takes about 0.07 s to arrive. Einstein's general theory of relativity was published in 1915 (he first predicted that clocks run slower where gravity is stronger in 1907).
