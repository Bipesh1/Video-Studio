# Example Video Script 03: What If the Sun Sent You a Bill?

## Production details

- **Niche:** "What if" science
- **Format:** An experience told in the second person. The viewer is the one who receives the bill.
- **Length:** About 2:45 of narration at 155 words per minute, which fills a 3:00 video once the silences are added
- **Narration word count:** 427
- **Sizes:** 1080x1920 for Facebook Reels, 1920x1080 for YouTube
- **Voice:** Quiet and close, like someone reading a letter aloud at night. Dry humour in the first half, then slower and warmer from Beat 6.
- **Palettes:** warm-interior for the room, storm-slate for the second page, clear-day for the ending

## Characters

- **You:** One stick figure in a small room at night. No name. The narration always says "you".
- **The Envelope:** A gold envelope that glows. It is the only light source in the room for most of the video, and it behaves like a character.

## Three devices that make this feel like an experience, not a lesson

1. **The light does the explaining.** Whatever the envelope's glow touches gets a small tag that reads "SUNLIGHT": the bread, the fuel can, the wall socket, the rain on the window. The narration never has to explain the idea. The viewer watches the room confess.
2. **A meter that never stops.** From the first second, a small counter in the corner reads "Sunlight delivered since you pressed play" and climbs at more than 8 billion dollars a second. Nobody mentions it. It ends the video at about 1.5 trillion dollars.
3. **The bill changes.** The page is the same sheet of paper throughout, but the viewer reads it three times: as a threat, as a puzzle, and finally as a gift.

## The script

### Beat 1. The envelope (0:00 to 0:15)

**Narration:** An envelope slides under your door. No stamp. It is warm. Inside is a bill, and the name at the top is yours. Sender: the Sun. Amount due, for the last hour of sunlight on Earth: thirty trillion dollars.

**On screen:** A dark room at night. A gold envelope slides under the door and lights the floor. You pick it up and pull your hand back from the heat. The page unfolds toward the camera: "FROM: THE SUN. FOR: ONE HOUR OF SUNLIGHT. AMOUNT DUE: $30,000,000,000,000". The corner meter starts counting.

**Sound:** Silence, then paper on a wooden floor.

**Caption emphasis:** Sender: the Sun, thirty trillion dollars

### Beat 2. The sum (0:15 to 0:46)

**Narration:** You laugh. Then you check the sum. Take all the sunlight that lands on this planet in one hour. Price it like the electricity in your wall. Thirty trillion. About what the whole United States earns in a year. Every four hours, the Sun delivers the value of everything the entire world earns in a year. And then it does it again. In under ninety minutes, the Earth soaks up more energy than every city, car and factory will use this year.

**On screen:** You sit at a table with a pencil. The glow from the page projects a small Earth onto the wall, half lit. A price tag swings from it. Beside it, a stack of coins labelled "USA, one year" rises to the same height as "Sun, one hour". Then a far taller stack labelled "World, one year" is matched by four small suns in a row.

**Sound:** A pencil scratching. A soft cash-register tick as each stack lands.

**Caption emphasis:** one hour, every four hours

### Beat 3. Your share (0:46 to 1:03)

**Narration:** You try the obvious defence. That is not all mine. Fine. Split it between every person alive. Your share is more than three thousand dollars. An hour. Thirty million dollars a year, delivered to you, for free, since the day you were born.

**On screen:** You push the bill away. The page slides back by itself. The big number breaks into billions of tiny coins that fly out of the window to tiny stick figures across a dark globe. One coin stream stays and pours onto your head. A second line appears on the page: "YOUR SHARE: $3,600 PER HOUR".

**Sound:** Coins, like rain on a roof.

**Caption emphasis:** thirty million dollars a year

### Beat 4. The real bills (1:03 to 1:27)

**Narration:** Then you look at your real bills. Electricity. Fuel. Food. And you notice something. Coal is sunlight that plants caught hundreds of millions of years ago. Oil is the same story. Wind is air the Sun warmed. Rain is water the Sun lifted. Your dinner is this summer's light. You have almost never paid for energy. You have only paid for delivery.

**On screen:** You carry the envelope around the room like a lamp. Each thing its glow touches grows a tag that reads "SUNLIGHT": a lump of coal, with a tiny ancient fern inside it. A fuel can. The curtain moving in the wind. Rain on the window. A loaf of bread. Your pile of ordinary bills on the table all turn gold at the edges.

**Sound:** One soft chime for each tag, rising in pitch.

**Caption emphasis:** only paid for delivery

### Beat 5. The savings account (1:27 to 1:42)

**Narration:** And the coal and the oil? That is a savings account. Sunlight, deposited a little at a time, over hundreds of millions of years. We found the account a few hundred years ago. We have been emptying it ever since.

**On screen:** The floor of the room becomes glass. Below it is a vast vault. A long clock spins while single gold coins drop in, one at a time, until the vault is full. Then a hatch opens and a crowd of stick figures rush in with sacks and wheelbarrows and carry the coins out far faster than they went in.

**Sound:** One slow coin drop, repeated. Then a rush of footsteps.

**Caption emphasis:** a savings account

### Beat 6. The second page (1:42 to 2:05)

**Narration:** So you turn the bill over, looking for the catch. There is a second page. It shows what this costs the sender. Every second, the Sun destroys four million tonnes of itself to make that light. And Earth catches almost none of it. One part in two billion. The rest pours into empty space, unbilled, unseen, for nobody.

**On screen:** You turn the page and the room falls away into space. The Sun fills the frame, with pieces of itself streaming off as light in every direction. The Earth is a single speck, catching one thin thread of light. Every other ray flies past into the dark. You stand on the page, very small, watching.

**Sound:** The music enters here for the first time: one low, slow note.

**Caption emphasis:** four million tonnes, for nobody

### Beat 7. The account (2:05 to 2:18)

**Narration:** It has done this for four and a half billion years. Before your street. Before people. Before anything alive could look up. It lit oceans with nothing in them. No invoice. No reminder. No interest.

**On screen:** The same patch of ground shown four times as the light stays constant: a street, an empty plain, a sea with no life, bare rock. The Sun sits in the same place in every frame.

**Sound:** The note holds.

**Caption emphasis:** No invoice. No interest.

### Beat 8. The last line (2:18 to 2:45)

**Narration:** At the bottom there is one last line, and it is not a demand. Amount due: nothing. It was never a bill. It was a statement. Paid in full, by the sender. Remaining credit: about five billion years. You fold it up. Outside, it is morning. And for the first time in your life, you feel the Sun on your face and know exactly what it is worth.

**On screen:** Back in the room. Close on the bottom of the page: "AMOUNT DUE: $0. PAID IN FULL BY SENDER. REMAINING CREDIT: 5,000,000,000 YEARS". You fold the page and open the door. Real sunlight replaces the envelope's glow and every tag in the room fades. You stand in the doorway with your face lifted. The corner meter stops at about $1.5 trillion, then fades too.

**Sound:** The music resolves. Birds. One beat after the last word, silence.

**Caption emphasis:** Paid in full, what it is worth

## Why this script is new

- **The idea is original.** It prices sunlight in dollars, which turns numbers nobody can picture into ones everybody understands.
- **The viewer is the main character.** Every line happens to "you", in the present tense, and nothing is defined or taught.
- **Each beat turns the idea over:** a bill you cannot pay, the sum is correct, your own share is a fortune, everything you already pay for is sunlight, the old sunlight is being spent fast, the sender is the one paying, and the bill was a gift.
- **The ending is a feeling.** The last line changes something the viewer does every day.
- **It could become a series.** The same device works for other things nature provides: "What if the ocean sent you a bill?" or "What if your heart sent you a bill?"

## Notes on accuracy

- **The dollar figures are my own calculations.** No source prices sunlight this way. They come from multiplying the sourced numbers above: 174 petawatts for one hour, at the US home electricity price, gives about 30 to 32 trillion dollars.
- **The comparisons:** That hourly figure is close to the IMF's forecast for one year of the US economy, and four hours of it is close to one year of the world economy.
- **Your share:** The hourly figure divided by 8.3 billion people is about 3,600 to 3,800 dollars an hour, or roughly 32 million dollars a year. The script rounds this to "more than three thousand" and "thirty million".
- **One part in two billion:** This is also my calculation, from the Earth's 174 petawatts divided by the Sun's total output. The exact result is about one part in 2.2 billion.
- **"All the sunlight that lands on this planet":** The 174 petawatt figure is measured at the top of the atmosphere. About 30 percent is reflected before it reaches the ground.
- **"Under ninety minutes":** The source says the energy the Earth absorbed in about one hour and 25 minutes matched a year of world energy use in 2019.
- **"A few hundred years ago":** This is my rounding for the start of large-scale coal use, not a sourced date.
- **"Almost never paid for energy":** The word "almost" matters. Nuclear and geothermal energy do not come from the Sun.
- **Four million tonnes:** The source gives 4 billion kilograms a second, which is 4 million tonnes.
- **The price is American.** Electricity costs differ by country, so the dollar figures would change with a different price.
- **Wikipedia sources:** Four of the eight sources are Wikipedia pages. Replace them with the primary sources they cite before publishing.
